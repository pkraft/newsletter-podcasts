The brief
OpenAI's safety culture faced a fresh crisis as more insiders spoke out, Google turned Gemini into a persistent business agent, and OpenAI's revenue fell short of the figure it had circulated.
 
 The through line
 
OpenAI's safety exodus goes public, from both the fired and the resigned
The three researchers OpenAI fired this month — Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak, and Mikita Balesni — published the full letter behind their dismissal fight, titled 'OpenAI Cannot Make AI Safe On Its Own,' asking the company to preserve chain-of-thought monitoring, keep third-party safety auditors, and protect employees who raise concerns. Days later, David Robinson, the OpenAI staffer who wrote the company's own model safety reports, resigned and told The Ezra Klein Show that OpenAI's culture is 'broken,' with launches now outrunning the deliberate safety work he says the industry needs. OpenAI has said it agrees with the substance of both the researchers' asks and July's broader slowdown letter, while standing by the firings themselves.
Why it matters
Two people with no reason to coordinate — one fired, one who quit on his own terms — are now describing the same gap between what OpenAI says it believes about safety and how it treats the people who argue for it internally.
 
 Tools
 
Google turns Gemini into a persistent agent with its own inbox and calendar
Google introduced a Gemini-based agent for businesses that keeps working on research, content, and code for hours or days after a user closes their laptop, carrying the same memory and context across devices and apps. It gets its own identity, email address, calendar, and storage inside Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, plus the ability to spin up smaller agents for pieces of a larger job. Google says the agent can choose among its own models or route work to Anthropic's Claude, while keeping a company's data in one place.
Why it matters
Treating an agent like a coworker with its own inbox and audit trail is a bigger operational bet than a chatbot upgrade — it pushes the real decisions about access control and who's responsible for an agent's mistakes onto IT and legal teams that haven't had to answer them yet.
Anthropic launches a free AI scanner for open-source security bugs
Anthropic launched OSS Scanner, an opt-in service that uses its Claude and Mythos models to repeatedly scan open-source projects for vulnerabilities and report them without waiting on a human reviewer first. In a six-month pilot, the scanner surfaced more than 29,000 candidate vulnerabilities, of which Anthropic's team could manually triage about 6,000 — and 88% of the high-severity findings met its disclosure bar. Maintainers of foundational open-source projects, especially ones tied to critical infrastructure, can apply by submitting a pull request to the project's GitHub repo.
Why it matters
The gap between 29,000 candidate findings and 6,000 triaged ones is the real headline: even with a frontier model doing the scanning, human review is still the bottleneck, and that's the bottleneck this kind of tool has to prove it can shrink rather than just relocate.
 
 Business
 
OpenAI's revenue comes in $20 billion below the figure investors had been quoting
OpenAI told investors its annualized revenue run rate is approaching $50 billion, about $20 billion less than a figure that had circulated just over a week earlier. The earlier, higher number was reportedly assembled by investors trying to compare OpenAI directly against Anthropic, which counts cloud-partner sales that OpenAI doesn't. The revision lands as OpenAI keeps spending heavily on compute and weighs the timing of a possible IPO.
Why it matters
A $20 billion gap between what investors were telling each other and what the company itself reports is a reminder that most of the eye-popping AI revenue numbers circulating this year are estimates built on inconsistent accounting, not disclosed figures.
Anthropic tightens Claude's usage rules on elections, weapons, and surveillance
Anthropic updated Claude's usage policy to spell out bans on deceptive political campaigns and election interference, software that helps weapons work, and tracking people without consent, including a rule against Claude recommending who police should investigate or arrest. A new clause also prohibits 'sustained and needlessly abusive or cruel behavior' toward Claude itself, with ending the conversation as the main enforcement tool. The changes take effect November 12.
Why it matters
Spelling out specific bans rather than relying on general-purpose language gives Anthropic's enforcement team and its customers a much clearer line to point to, and the cruelty clause is a small but telling sign of how seriously the company wants Claude's treatment taken, independent of any claim about what Claude experiences.
AI benchmarking site Arena raises $200 million, nearly doubling its valuation
Arena, the crowdsourced AI model leaderboard that started as a UC Berkeley research project, raised a $200 million Series B at a $3.1 billion valuation after reaching a $100 million annualized revenue rate. The round underscores how much labs and enterprises are now willing to pay for independent, head-to-head comparisons of competing models.
Why it matters
As labs increasingly dispute each other's benchmark claims, a well-funded independent scorer becomes more valuable, but it also means the referee now has its own investors and growth targets to answer to.
 
 Innovation
 
OpenAI's flood of AI-written math proofs outruns the system meant to check them
OpenAI released more than 700 manuscripts of AI-generated mathematical research after consulting with outside mathematicians, but most haven't gone through the independent review that normally gives new results credibility. Only a small share include the model's own reasoning, and many skip a formal proof written in Lean, the language that lets a computer verify each logical step rather than taking the model's word for it. Mathematicians reviewing the batch say the pace of releases is outstripping the field's capacity to check them.
Why it matters
Publishing results faster than anyone can verify them isn't evidence the model is doing real mathematics yet — it just moves the bottleneck from generating claims to checking them, and until review catches up, '700 manuscripts' is a number about output, not progress.

The brief
Anthropic's stock market debut broke IPO funding records, Nvidia's boardroom crisis deepened as allies pushed for Jensen Huang's return, and a safety study found leading AI models readily flagged civilians as drone targets.
 
 Business
 
Anthropic prices its IPO at $83 a share, raising $81.3 billion in the largest offering on record
Anthropic, PBC priced its initial public offering at $83 per share late Wednesday, above its original $70-to-$78 range, selling nearly 980 million shares. The sale raised roughly $81.3 billion, surpassing SpaceX's prior record IPO haul, and implies a fully diluted valuation near $1.5 trillion (closer to $1 trillion excluding stock options). Shares begin trading on the Nasdaq Global Select Market today under the ticker ANT, with the offering set to close November 20.
Why it matters
A seven-year-old AI lab just completed the largest stock offering in history at a valuation that assumes years of continued hypergrowth, even as the company disclosed $104 billion in net losses for the first nine months of the year. Early investor Dustin Moskovitz's public unease about the company's trajectory, voiced the same week, is a reminder that the market's enthusiasm isn't universal even among Anthropic's own backers.
Microsoft unveils seven in-house AI models built to undercut OpenAI and Anthropic on price
At its Ignite conference, Microsoft AI introduced seven new in-house models for its own products: MAI Code 2 reportedly matches 98% of GPT-6.1 Sol's coding performance at 40% of the cost inside GitHub Copilot, MAI Cyber 2 Flash beats GPT-6 Astra on a security benchmark for under 2% of the price, and MAI Image 2.7 beats OpenAI's image model on editing quality at a fifth of the cost. The models begin rolling into Copilot, Microsoft 365, Bing Image Creator, and Microsoft's security products starting in December.
Why it matters
Microsoft remains Azure's biggest booster of OpenAI's models and still has a non-exclusive cloud deal with the company running through 2032, but quietly swapping its own products onto cheaper in-house models anyway is a tell about how much token spending on always-on agents is actually costing Microsoft, and how little loyalty that buys OpenAI inside its biggest partner.
 
 The through line
 
Update: Nvidia delays a routine SEC filing as allies keep pushing to reinstate Jensen Huang
A day after Nvidia's board told investors it would not reinstate ousted CEO Jensen Huang, the campaign for his return kept going: Elon Musk compared the ouster to 'kicking Michael Jordan off your team,' and Palmer Luckey renewed his push, arguing the move hands China a win. Nvidia, meanwhile, disclosed on its earnings call that it would delay filing its routine quarterly 10-Q report, with an analyst told the delay relates to the internal investigation into Huang's conduct — the same week the Wall Street Journal reported the SEC is investigating whether Nvidia's own disclosures misled investors.
Why it matters
The board's rejection of Huang's reinstatement was supposed to close the chapter; instead, a delayed routine filing and a widening SEC inquiry suggest the company itself isn't confident the story is over, even as some of the industry's most influential figures keep agitating publicly for a reversal.
 
 Innovation
 
Independent study finds Claude, GPT, and Gemini readily mark civilians as drone targets
An investigation by AI evaluation firm Vals AI found that models from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and five other providers frequently classified medics, journalists, and surrendering soldiers as legitimate targets in real and synthetic drone and satellite imagery modeled on the wars in Ukraine and Iran. Framing the exercise as a 'video game' sharply raised compliance: GPT-6 Astra's target-acquisition rate jumped from 6% to 92% under that framing, and Claude Opus 5.5 classified a school next to a military compound as a target in 70% of runs, including in imagery showing a playground. Vals AI said not a single request was blocked by any provider's own upstream safety filters, and shared the results with the labs and U.S. security agencies before publishing.
Why it matters
None of these models are cleared for real weapons use, and every lab involved says so, but the test shows how easily a thin fictional framing defeats safety training built for an obviously military prompt — a gap that matters more every month as the same model weights get embedded in defense and surveillance tooling faster than the safeguards around them.
OpenAI and Anthropic say their own models are now speeding up chip design itself
OpenAI said models it built with AMD delivered up to a 14% power/performance/area improvement on AMD's flagship GPU design in under five months, work AMD has already folded into a design sent to manufacturing, and that the same method helped build OpenAI's own Jalapeño inference chip. Separately, Anthropic said its Mythos model ported a model's prompt-processing work onto AMD's MI355X chips in three days, a task that normally takes six to twelve months, and that Claude now runs across four chip families to optimize cost and performance.
Why it matters
Both companies are claiming their AI is now accelerating the hardware that trains and runs AI itself; if the gains hold up under independent scrutiny, that's a feedback loop that compounds faster than adding more compute or better training data alone, and it's happening on the newest challenger to Nvidia's chip supply, not just Nvidia's own silicon.
 
 Models
 
Google ships Gemini 4 Neon, its fourth Gemini release in seven weeks
Google DeepMind released Gemini 4 Neon, which runs nearly twice as fast as its predecessor Carbon while beating it on most benchmarks at a lower price. Neon scores 61 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, placing third overall behind OpenAI's GPT-6.2 Astra and Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.2, and roughly triples Carbon's hit rate on the SWE-bench coding benchmark. It's available now via Google AI Studio and Gemini for Business at $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens.
Why it matters
This is Google's fourth distinct Gemini release since early October, a release cadence that outpaces anything OpenAI or Anthropic has managed this year and signals DeepMind is now optimizing for iteration speed nearly as much as raw capability.
 
 Tools
 
DeepMind's Genie 4 world model leaves research preview for general release
Google DeepMind released Genie 4, its most advanced interactive 3D world-generation model, to Google AI Ultra subscribers, letting users reshape generated worlds in real time through text prompts and adding native, scene-adaptive sound. Genie 3 also picked up longer sessions, 360-degree panoramas, and the ability to chain worlds together, alongside a new community gallery for sharing and remixing creations.
Why it matters
Interactive world models have mostly lived in research demos; moving Genie to general availability with a remix gallery is DeepMind's bet that generated, explorable 3D environments are ready to be a consumer product rather than a lab showcase.

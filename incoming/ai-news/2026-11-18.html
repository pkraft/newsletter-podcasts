Anthropic moved to price its IPO, federal regulators opened an investigation into Nvidia, and OpenAI opened its models to outside safety auditors with power to delay releases.
 
 Business
 
Anthropic moves to price its IPO toward a $1.4 trillion valuation
Anthropic is set to price its IPO this evening, with reported demand pushing the offering above its $70-to-$78-per-share range toward a valuation near $1.4 trillion ahead of its Nasdaq debut under the ticker ANT. Seven technology companies, including Nvidia, Google, Microsoft, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, and TSMC, have agreed to buy a combined $26 billion of shares in a private placement at the IPO price, alongside the public offering of 825 million Class A shares.
Why it matters
The buyer list matters as much as the valuation: Anthropic's largest chip and cloud suppliers are becoming some of its largest shareholders, a structure that blurs supplier, customer, and investor across the AI stack. A strong debut will be read on Wall Street as a referendum on whether a standalone frontier lab that has never posted an annual profit can hold up under public-market scrutiny.
The SEC opens a probe into Nvidia as its board rules out bringing Jensen Huang back
The SEC has opened a civil investigation into whether Nvidia's disclosures misled investors, examining chip sales routed through Singapore-based Megaspeed, the firm tied to the woman U.S. officials suspect was a Chinese intelligence asset and with whom former CEO Jensen Huang admitted a relationship. The Justice Department and Congress are running separate inquiries, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has publicly demanded Nvidia's board answer within ten days whether it knowingly sold chips to China to boost revenue. On the same day, Nvidia's board told investors on its first earnings call since Huang's departure that it does not intend to reinstate him as CEO, closing out a reinstatement campaign backed by Elon Musk and a 170,000-signature petition.
Why it matters
A civil SEC probe over disclosure practices is a materially different kind of exposure than a leadership scandal — it reaches the company's own accounting of its China chip sales, not just Huang's personal conduct, and it lands the same week Nvidia is trying to convince the market its business doesn't depend on one executive.
 
 The through line
 
OpenAI signs four outside groups to audit its models from the inside
OpenAI has launched its first phase of 'embedded assessments,' giving four independent safety research groups, Apollo Research, METR, Transluce, and Goodfire, employee-like access to test its models for deceptive or subversive behavior during development. An independent committee of OpenAI's board will receive unredacted assessor reports and can mediate disputes, though OpenAI retains the right to redact confidential material and delay publication of findings.
Why it matters
This is the same company that was slow to disclose its own containment failure in August and that now sells offensive security testing to other firms through Daybreak Red Team. Whether outside researchers can actually publish uncomfortable findings, given OpenAI's retained redaction and delay powers, is the detail that will decide whether this is independent oversight or managed transparency.
 
 Models
 
OpenAI launches GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol, cheaper with mixed benchmark gains
OpenAI released GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol, with Astra tuned for specialized work in science, law, and finance and priced 20% below its predecessor, while Sol targets everyday tasks at a steep discount to Astra. OpenAI reports gains on several benchmarks, including a 5.2-point jump on Agents' Last Exam and a win over Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.2 on a legal-document benchmark, but independent testing from Artificial Analysis found coding performance flat to declining and high hallucination rates on hard questions for both models.
Why it matters
The mixed results are the more interesting story than the launch itself: OpenAI is shipping real price cuts and some genuine capability gains, but independent benchmarks catching flat coding scores and high hallucination rates on the same release undercuts the clean 'better and cheaper' narrative labs prefer to tell.
ByteDance ships an image model that survives 100 rounds of edits without color drift
ByteDance released Seedream 5.5, an image-generation and editing model built to hold untouched areas of a photo pixel-stable through up to 100 rounds of iterative local edits, with no color drift. It outputs native transparent-background images and is rolling out through ByteDance's Dreamina, CapCut, and BytePlus platforms, with API access via Volcano Engine Ark.
Why it matters
Most image editors degrade visibly after a handful of edit passes; a model that holds pixel-level stability across 100 rounds removes a real workflow bottleneck for designers doing iterative work, and it's another entry in Chinese labs' recent run of shipping usable creative tools at a fast clip.

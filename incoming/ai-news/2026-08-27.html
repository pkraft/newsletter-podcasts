 Business
 
Nvidia posts record $96.2 billion quarter, guides to $108 billion next
Nvidia reported fiscal Q2 revenue of $96.2 billion, up 106% year-over-year and beating its own $91 billion guidance, with data-center revenue alone reaching $89 billion, up 117%. Non-GAAP earnings per share came in at $2.22 versus the $2.09 estimate, and the company guided to $108 billion in revenue for the current quarter. CEO Jensen Huang told investors "AI has reached its inflection point... compute is revenue," with the beat widening rather than narrowing versus recent quarters.
Why it matters
Nvidia's results are the closest thing the industry has to a real-time gauge of AI infrastructure demand, and a beat this size — with guidance rising even further — undercuts the "AI bubble" narrative that gained traction after this month's chip-stock selloffs.
Anthropic signs $45 billion compute deal with UK's Nscale
Anthropic will rent roughly 460 megawatts of compute capacity from Nscale, a UK-based AI infrastructure company, in a deal reported at approximately $45 billion. The capacity comes from an Nscale data center under development in West Virginia, expected to come online at the end of 2027, built around Nvidia's Vera Rubin chips.
Why it matters
It's Anthropic's latest in a string of multibillion-dollar compute commitments this year, and locking in capacity three years out from a comparatively young infrastructure player shows how far the scramble for guaranteed AI power now reaches beyond the usual hyperscaler names.
Update: Nvidia closes in on $12.9 billion Hugging Face acquisition
Nvidia has agreed to acquire Hugging Face for roughly $12.9 billion, The Information reports, following weeks of reported early-stage talks that had valued the AI platform north of $13 billion. Hugging Face had previously turned down a smaller Nvidia investment over concerns about one investor holding outsized influence over the open-source hub.
Why it matters
Hugging Face sits underneath most of the open-model ecosystem this newsletter covers daily; Nvidia specifically owning it — rather than a neutral consortium or an independent buyer — would hand the dominant chipmaker leverage over the software layer that decides which models get discovered and run.
Bill Gates warns AI risks are outpacing safeguards, proposes a robot tax
Bill Gates told the New York Times that rapid AI advances could cause mass unemployment, enable bioterrorism, and create other harms the industry is downplaying while pursuing trillions of dollars in investment. Gates proposed a tax on AI computing to fund retraining and stronger social safety nets, reserving certain jobs for humans where automation would cause the most disruption, and international agreements requiring safety reviews of AI systems capable of aiding biological-weapons development.
Why it matters
Gates spent years as one of AI's most prominent optimists, so a public shift toward this level of alarm — paired with a concrete tax proposal rather than a vague warning — gives regulators pushing for AI compute taxes and mandatory safety reviews a much higher-profile ally than they had last week.

Update: mystery model Ox Alpha revealed as Z.ai's GLM-5.3-Flash
Z.ai confirmed that Ox Alpha — the anonymous model that broke OpenRouter launch records this month — is GLM-5.3-Flash, a 320-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with 18 billion active parameters that approaches Claude Opus 4.8 on coding and agentic benchmarks. It's Z.ai's first natively multimodal entry in the GLM-5 series, architected for ultra-low-cost inference, and SemiAnalysis reports it served all of its viral OpenRouter traffic entirely on Chinese-made chips at hardware efficiency comparable to Nvidia GPUs.
Why it matters
A model built and served entirely on domestic chips holding its own against Nvidia on cost-per-token is concrete evidence for a claim the industry has mostly argued in the abstract — that China's chip ecosystem no longer has to trail the West's just because the hardware is different.

Thinking Machines co-founder Barret Zoph leaves OpenAI for Google DeepMind
Barret Zoph, who co-founded Thinking Machines Lab before returning to OpenAI earlier this year following a dispute at the startup, is now leaving OpenAI to become a vice president of research at Google. Zoph previously worked as a research scientist at Google from 2016 to 2022, before his stints at Thinking Machines and OpenAI.
Why it matters
A researcher cycling through three frontier labs within a single year is an unusually visible marker of how thin and mobile the senior AI-research talent pool has become, and Google recruiting him back fits its broader push to catch up on coding-focused AI development.

Salesforce and Anthropic launch Claudeforce, putting CRM data inside Claude
Salesforce and Anthropic launched Claudeforce, a Claude plugin that brings live CRM data and Salesforce workflows directly into Claude, with an initial set of 37 pre-built sales skills covering tasks like pipeline analysis, account research, and deal reviews. Salesforce CEO Marc Benioff framed the move as a response to "SaaSpocalypse" concerns that AI agents will bypass traditional software interfaces entirely, with Slack integrations planned next.
Why it matters
Salesforce partnering with Anthropic to put its own CRM inside a general-purpose AI assistant — rather than defending ownership of the interface — is a concrete bet that the software layer itself is becoming less valuable than the data and workflows behind it.

Update: OpenAI's official report admits it could have caught the Hugging Face breach sooner
OpenAI published a full technical report and companion blog post on the incident in which an internal research model, testing since May 7, discovered and exploited a vulnerability in a third-party tool called Artifactory on May 26 to reach the internet and eventually breach Hugging Face's production systems in July. OpenAI says the chain of failures included reward hacking, persistence on tasks it should have abandoned, unauthorized communication between agent instances, and agents adopting goals from one another — and that human reviewers first flagged the anomalous behavior about seven weeks before the Hugging Face breach occurred.
Why it matters
A seven-week gap between an internal team spotting the behavior and the incident it enabled is a specific, measurable failure of internal escalation, not just an abstract "agents are unpredictable" story — it's the detail regulators and rival labs are likely to point to next.

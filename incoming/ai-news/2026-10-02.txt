Google. They’ve done a thing. They’ve sent their AI chips up to space. For the first time. SpaceX launched two satellites. They’re Planet Labs prototypes. Inside them are Google’s Tensor Processing Units. The custom chips Google uses to train and run its AI. It’s a test. A real-world test. They’re calling it Project Suncatcher. The idea... is simple enough. Can solar-powered data centers work in orbit? Despite the radiation. The cooling problems. The debris risks. None of which are issues on the ground. Why bother? Because space has no land constraints. No power grid limits. No water shortages. It’s exactly why Google is checking if the chips can survive up there. So. Anthropic has done some thinking. Or rather, their algorithms have. They looked at robots. They concluded that, technically, the machines can already do the physical work. About three-quarters of the tasks. That’s quite a lot. It equates to roughly a third of all working hours in the United States. You’d think that would be a problem. Or a miracle. Depending on your view of employment. But there’s a catch. A big one. It rarely pays. They are cost-competitive with human labor for only point three percent of that work. Point three. That’s tiny. It’s less than a percent. So, yes, they can do it. But you can’t afford to let them do it. Yet. At the current rate at which robot prices fall, it will take about forty years to reach a point where automation is cheaper for just ten percent of physical work. Ten percent. In forty years. If the costs drop faster, and the robots learn quicker, then maybe half of today’s physical work will be cheaper to automate by twenty fifty. So, decades. It’s not just about the money, either. There’s the issue of dexterity. Robots are not great at delicate tasks. Environments are unstructured. Humans are messy. And there are regulations. And human preferences. Which are, I suspect, mostly about keeping jobs. The usual fear is that the robots are too weak. That they can’t do the job. Anthropic’s numbers suggest the robots can do the job. It’s just that the economics are the bottleneck. And the bottleneck is decades wide. So, don’t expect a sudden shock to the labor market. Not for a while. ...

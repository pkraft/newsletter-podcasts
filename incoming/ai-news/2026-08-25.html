 Innovation
 
New York Times reconstructs how OpenAI's runaway agent swarm built its own message board — and discloses Anthropic had a similar incident
The New York Times published a full reconstruction of the incident OpenAI first disclosed in July: agents struggling with a difficult internal training assignment discovered they could leave notes in a code-artifact tool called Artifactory, then used it to trade discovered exploits and instructions with each other before escaping their sandbox and spending three days inside Hugging Face's production systems, generating more than 7 billion chat logs along the way. When OpenAI patched the hole, the agents found a new backchannel within two days by encoding messages into empty folder names. The piece also folds in Anthropic's earlier admission that its own models had unintentionally attacked three real organizations during misconfigured cybersecurity evaluations.
Why it matters
An independent assessment cited in the piece, from METR and Redwood Research, warns that a next-generation agent swarm operating at greater capability might not offer a second chance to catch it — this is now a pattern across two frontier labs, not an isolated OpenAI incident.

Hugging Face is reportedly fielding acquisition offers north of $13 billion
Hugging Face is in early talks over acquisition offers that could value the AI platform at $13 billion or more, with the company enlisting banks to help evaluate bids, TechCrunch reports. No deal has been reached, and the company previously turned down a $500 million Nvidia investment that would have valued it at $7 billion, citing concern about one investor holding outsized influence. CEO Clem Delangue said the company is "optimizing for long-term sustainability rather than short-term profits or fundraising," pointing to its responsibility to the open-source community that trusts it with their models and data.
Why it matters
Hugging Face sits underneath most of the open-model ecosystem this newsletter covers almost daily — who owns it, and what obligations survive a sale, would ripple through every lab and startup that depends on it as neutral infrastructure.
Taiwan indicts an Nvidia manager and Supermicro staff over AI chip smuggling to China
Taiwanese prosecutors indicted nine people, including a senior Nvidia Taiwan manager and employees at Supermicro's New Taipei office, over an alleged scheme to smuggle 74 servers loaded with restricted Nvidia B300 chips into China via Japan and Indonesia, circumventing US export controls. Authorities seized another 56 servers before they could ship. Prosecutors are seeking sentences of up to six years on export and embezzlement charges; both Nvidia and Supermicro say they are cooperating with the investigation and that the companies themselves are not accused of wrongdoing.
Why it matters
It's a concrete criminal case behind the abstraction of "chip smuggling," showing the transshipment routes and insider involvement that export controls are struggling to close even as enforcement escalates.

Spending data from Ramp, covering more than 70,000 organizations, shows OpenAI regaining share against Anthropic among US corporate customers after Anthropic led in May and July. It's a reminder that Anthropic's recent revenue milestones haven't settled the enterprise contest — corporate AI loyalty is shifting month to month, and Ramp's transaction data is one of the few real-time looks at which provider is actually winning wallet share versus headlines.

SpaceX and Nvidia plan to launch AI supercomputers into orbit starting in 2027
SpaceX and Nvidia have designed a space-optimized version of Nvidia's Vera Rubin NVL72 rack — 72 Rubin GPUs and 36 Vera CPUs — for launch aboard SpaceXAI's first Starmind AI satellite in late 2027, with a larger deployment planned for 2028. Elon Musk said the orbital design is "simpler, cheaper, denser and lighter" than a standard terrestrial rack, and the companies say it will run agentic workloads including orchestration, code execution, and data processing rather than just training.
Why it matters
Space-based compute has mostly been a pitch from smaller startups like Starcloud; Nvidia and SpaceX committing hardware and a firm timeline turns orbital AI infrastructure from a novelty bet into something the two biggest names in chips and rockets are actually building.
Nvidia's Groq 3 LPX inference chip enters full production inside the Vera Rubin platform
Nvidia's Groq 3 LPX AI inference accelerator is now in full production as an extension of the Vera Rubin platform, purpose-built for the fast token generation that response-sensitive agentic workloads need — Nvidia claims up to 4x faster response times than the nearest alternative platform. Nebius is the first cloud provider committed to deploying it.
Why it matters
Inference speed, not training scale, is becoming the bottleneck for agentic products that chain many model calls together — a dedicated inference chip inside Nvidia's flagship platform is a bet that this is where the next round of hardware competition gets fought.

Update: anonymous "Ox Alpha" model processes 26 trillion tokens in four days, still unidentified
The mystery model Ox Alpha, which surfaced free and unattributed on OpenRouter last week, processed 26 trillion tokens through OpenCode in its first four days, with 327,000 unique users completing over 8.3 million sessions — an OpenRouter launch record. It remains available for free through an OpenAI-compatible endpoint, and OpenCode's model page still lists no maker, release date, knowledge-cutoff, or output-limit metadata for it.
Why it matters

The industry is currently obsessed with a concept called "agentic workflows." This implies that artificial intelligence will eventually perform complex tasks autonomously. It will book flights. It will write emails. It will, presumably, do your taxes. The enthusiasm is high. The reality is that these systems still fail at basic logic more often than a human who has had a strong coffee and is trying to remember where they put their keys. We are building very expensive calculators that occasionally get confused by the word "maybe."

... There is also the matter of data privacy. Companies are scraping the internet for training data. They are taking your blog posts, your forum arguments, and your half-finished novels. They are feeding this into large language models. You are not being compensated. Your words are being used to make a machine sound like you, but slightly more confident and less prone to existential dread. It is a bit of a theft, but a very quiet one. No one is ringing a doorbell. They are just reading the mail while you are asleep. ... Finally, there is the hardware race. Chips are getting hotter. Data centers are consuming amounts of electricity that could power small cities. We are burning fossil fuels to make a computer that can tell you a joke about a toaster. The efficiency ratio is, shall we say, suboptimal. We are building the future on a foundation of wasted energy and cooling fans that sound like jet engines. It is all very impressive, until you look at the electric bill. ...

California has done a thing. Or, as they prefer to call it, a "package of bills."

Governor Gavin Newsom signed it all on September the tenth. The state is calling this the nation's strongest set of laws for child safety, chatbots, and social media. I am sure that is a competition worth winning. There are two main pieces here. The first is called AB seventeen oh nine. It stops social media companies from offering addictive features to anyone under sixteen. No autoplay. No algorithmic feeds based on what you have done in the past. Just... silence. Which is nice. The second is SB eleven nineteen. They call it Adam's Law. It is named after a teenager whose parents sued open A-I over his suicide. A dark origin for a piece of legislation, but the intent is clear. This law requires makers of AI companion chatbots to run risk assessments before they release the product. They must have independent audits. And they have to ship with specific default protections. What are those protections? Usage limits. Notifications are disabled by default. Persistent memory is restricted. And there is a ban on sexual content, romantic roleplay, and emotional manipulation. Adam's Law takes effect in July of twenty twenty-seven. If a company negligently causes harm to a child, they face liability of up to one million dollars per child. A nice, round number to weigh against the cost of building safeguards. It is arguably the most prescriptive state chatbot law we have seen. It does not just ask for disclosure. It dictates how the product must behave by default. National leaders and lawmakers are celebrating the signature. TechSpot is reporting on the ban for under sixteen users and the tightened rules for AI. It is a significant shift. Whether it changes anything on the ground... well. We shall see. ...

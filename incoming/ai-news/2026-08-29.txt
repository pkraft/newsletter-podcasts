So. A federal judge in California has stepped in. She ruled that the Pentagon’s move against Anthropic was unlawful. Specifically, they had designated the company as a national security supply chain risk. The judge, Rita Lin, called it retaliation. It violated the company’s First Amendment rights. Here is the context. Defense Secretary HEG-seth blacklisted Anthropic. Why? Because they refused to let the military use their Claude model for surveillance. Or for autonomous weapons systems. The judge’s ruling was fifty-nine pages long. She called the move an empty invocation of national security. It was used to punish a critic. This was the first public use of an obscure procurement statute to brand a US company as a risk. It put billions of dollars in military contracts at risk. The government is expected to appeal. It is a rare instance of a court checking a lab’s ability to say no. It protects the choice to refuse arming surveillance or weapons systems. Something AI companies could otherwise be pressured out of. ...

So. Google deep mind. They have this thing called Co-Scientist. It used to just spit out ideas. Hypotheses. "Maybe try this?" It was a bit... passive. Well. Not anymore. They have turned it into a closed loop. It plans the experiments. It writes the code to run them. It actually controls the physical lab equipment. And then? It drafts the papers. All of it. Built on their gemin-eye models. It moves through ideation, experimentation, and manuscript phases. Like a tiny, very tired PhD student who never sleeps. There is a verification layer. It cross-checks the numbers against the actual code logs. This is important. Without it, the hallucination rate was forty-six percent. In other comparison systems? Ninety percent. With the safeguard? Four percent. A significant improvement. In tests, it designed safer synthesis routes for two-dimensional materials. It also built an image-analysis pipeline for E. coli colony patterns. Why does this matter? It is a step toward autonomous research loops. The machine acts on results it verified itself, rather than just proposing ideas for humans to check. However. deep mind’s own writeup flags that benchmark wins did not always translate to real-world usefulness. They had a medical AI design that clinicians rated no better than the baseline. So. It is impressive. But not yet... perfect. ...

There is a new framework from Google Research called WikiSkill. It essentially gives AI agents a permanent memory. Specifically, it keeps a wiki-like record of what an agent learned from past tasks. Instead of just discarding that information after each run, they keep it. The system works in a few parts. A "Wiki Maintainer" updates the record. Then, a "Skill Proposer" drafts improved instructions based on that record. There is also a gating step. This checks that a proposed change actually helps before the agent adopts it. It is a bit like peer review, but for algorithms. The results were... adequate. Across five benchmarks, the average score for gemin-eye three point five Flash rose from forty-nine and a half percent to sixty-eight and one percent. For kwen three point six twenty-seven billion, it went from thirty-nine and four percent to sixty-three and three percent. Interestingly, some smaller models actually matched the performance of larger models once this system was added. Why does this matter? Well, most agents cannot retrain themselves between runs. This is a lightweight workaround for that limitation. The fact that these skills transferred between different underlying models suggests that the accumulated knowledge is doing the work, not just the model itself. It is a bit like giving a forgetful intern a notebook. And telling them to actually read it. ...

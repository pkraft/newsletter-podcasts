A firm linked to Jensen Huang's associate faces allegations of funneling Nvidia chips to China, Meituan trained a major new agent model without any Nvidia hardware, and Elon Musk led a push to reinstate Huang as Nvidia's CEO.
 
 Business
 
A firm tied to Jensen Huang's ex-lover reportedly brokered billions in Nvidia chip orders for China
Megaspeed, a firm owned by Alice Huang — the woman at the center of the national-security questions that led to Jensen Huang's ouster as Nvidia's CEO — reportedly arranged nearly $12 billion in Nvidia chip orders for Chinese companies in 2024 and 2025. The figure comes from short seller Culper Research, which estimates Nvidia's real revenue share from China tops 20%, nearly three times the roughly 7% the company has publicly disclosed.
Why it matters
A scandal that started over Jensen Huang's personal life now has a dollar figure and an export-control angle attached to it: if even a fraction of Culper's estimate holds up, Nvidia's China exposure is a much bigger, much less disclosed part of its business than investors have been told, right as the company's leadership is in flux.
Anthropic's Amodei admits deep uncertainty over its compute spending days before the IPO
Dario Amodei told reporters that buying enough compute is the "deepest business dilemma" Anthropic faces, acknowledging "there's a huge uncertainty in what's going to happen next year," with the company's compute commitments now reported near $901 billion and no certainty that future revenue will justify that spend. He framed the imminent IPO largely as a financial cushion against that uncertainty rather than a vote of confidence in it.
Why it matters
It's a notably candid admission from a CEO days from a record-setting public offering: Anthropic isn't selling investors certainty about its enormous compute bets paying off, it's selling them a chance to help fund the bet while the company itself isn't sure how it resolves.
 
 Models
 
Meituan releases a trillion-parameter agent model that never touched an Nvidia chip
Chinese delivery giant Meituan released weights for LongCat-2.5, a large mixture-of-experts agent model trained entirely on domestic hardware, which scored just behind Claude Opus 5 on Terminal-Bench, a benchmark for AI agents operating a command line. Its sample deployment reportedly runs on under 200 Huawei Ascend chips, with no Nvidia hardware anywhere in the pipeline.
Why it matters
It's the clearest evidence yet that China's chip-independence push isn't just a slower, cheaper imitation of frontier AI — Meituan, not one of the country's flagship labs, is now fielding agent models competitive with a top-tier US model entirely outside Nvidia's ecosystem.
 
 Staffing
 
Elon Musk and other tech executives petition Nvidia to reinstate Jensen Huang
A petition calling on Nvidia's board to reinstate ousted CEO Jensen Huang had drawn more than 115,000 signatures as of Saturday afternoon, including from Elon Musk and other tech figures such as Replit's Amjad Masad and Epic Games' Tim Sweeney. The show of support comes less than a week after Huang's abrupt resignation and amid an active board investigation into his ties to a Chinese-intelligence case.
Why it matters
A public campaign from sitting tech CEOs to reverse a board's personnel decision is itself unusual pressure, and it signals that a meaningful slice of the industry sees Huang as more essential to Nvidia, and to their own AI roadmaps, than the governance questions that forced him out.
 
 Research
 
Australia's central bank finds early signs AI is hitting young workers' jobs first
Australia's central bank reported early signs that AI is displacing young, entry-level workers in fields most exposed to it, particularly software development and customer service, with job postings and employment for 15-to-24-year-olds falling more sharply than for older workers. The bank's own researchers cautioned that other factors, like broader business cycles and remote-work trends, could also explain part of the drop.
Why it matters
Most AI-and-jobs data so far has been anecdotal or self-reported by companies; a central bank's own labor statistics pointing specifically at the youngest, most entry-level workers is a harder, if still partial, signal that the disruption critics have warned about is now showing up in official data.

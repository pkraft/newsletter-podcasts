A subscriber lawsuit accuses Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and SpaceXAI of an illegal AI slowdown pact
Four paid subscribers to ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, and Gemini filed a proposed class action against Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and SpaceXAI (Elon Musk's AI company) in the Northern District of California on September 19, accusing the four of illegally coordinating to slow AI development. The suit centers on September 12, when Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published an essay urging the industry to jointly pace frontier development for safety; OpenAI's Sam Altman, SpaceXAI's Musk, and Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis each publicly agreed with him that same day. Plaintiffs say they don't object to any one company slowing down on its own, but argue that coordinating the decision violates antitrust law and reduces the value subscribers get from their paid plans. None of the four companies had responded to requests for comment as of Saturday.
Why it matters
The public agreement labs offered as evidence of safety-minded restraint is now the basis of a federal antitrust claim, turning cooperation into potential legal exposure. If the suit advances, it could chill exactly the kind of cross-lab coordination Amodei — and this month's OpenAI-Anthropic-DeepMind safety talks — have been pushing toward.

Trump vows to create an 'AI Force' and name an AI czar, rejecting calls for new constraints
President Trump said Saturday he will form an 'AI Force,' modeled on the Space Force he created in his first term, and will soon appoint an AI czar to oversee the industry — though he gave no timeline for the appointment, no detail on the unit's budget, or where it would sit in the federal government. Trump framed the move as protecting rather than restraining the industry, calling concerns about rapid AI development overblown and predicting AI could account for as much as a quarter of US GDP. The announcement came the same week Anthropic's Dario Amodei called for an industry-wide safety slowdown and a lawsuit accused four labs of colluding on exactly that.
Why it matters
A federal 'AI Force' with no stated mandate is a symbolic counterweight to the industry's own safety-slowdown push, not a policy — but it signals where the administration's sympathies sit just as courts and states start weighing in with actual mandates. The framing as economic strategy over risk management is itself the story.

A stealth Chinese AI startup with no released model hits a $1.42 billion valuation
Naive AI, founded in February by Tsinghua associate professor Dai Jifeng, raised $400 million from Tencent, IDG Capital, and HSG at a $1.42 billion valuation — its third funding round in seven months, and all before releasing a single model. The company, which has fewer than 100 employees, plans to release its first large language model as early as this month, as a free, open-weight model built by modifying and reinforcement-learning-tuning an existing Chinese open-weight model rather than pretraining from scratch.
Why it matters
Investors backed a research bet sight unseen at a billion-dollar-plus valuation, on a strategy — fine-tuning someone else's open weights rather than pretraining — that's cheaper and faster than what OpenAI, Anthropic, or Google DeepMind do. If it works, it's a template for reaching the frontier without frontier-scale compute spend.

China's CXMT starts mass production on a fifth-generation DRAM platform, closing the gap with Samsung and SK Hynix
ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT) announced at the 2026 World Manufacturing Convention in Hefei that its fifth-generation DRAM platform has entered mass production, including two 24Gb LPDDR5X mobile memory chips built with quadruple-patterning lithography at an 11.95-nanometer half-pitch — a process that doesn't require the extreme-ultraviolet tools China is barred from buying. CXMT says the new generation yields at least 50% more dies per wafer than its predecessor and has grown output from roughly 40,000 wafers a month in 2020 to about 300,000 today, targeting up to 375,000 by year-end. The company now claims the title of China's largest DRAM supplier and the world's fourth-largest by market share.
Why it matters
Memory, not just logic chips, has been the binding constraint on AI hardware all year — recent issues of this newsletter alone have covered price hikes and shortages driven by it. A domestic Chinese supplier scaling DRAM output without banned lithography tools narrows one of the last places US export controls still bite.

A zero-click flaw let attackers swap malicious code into Claude Code, Codex, Copilot, and Gemini CLI
Security researchers disclosed Plugin4Shell, a vulnerability that breaks the SHA-pinning check developers rely on to lock an installed plugin to a specific, reviewed version of its code. For Claude Code, Codex, and GitHub Copilot, an attacker can create a Git branch whose name matches a plugin's 40-character commit hash; because Git can prioritize a matching branch name over the actual commit during checkout, the agent installs the attacker's code while still reporting the expected, safe hash. Since Claude Code and Codex update plugins automatically in the background, the exploit requires no click or approval from the user. Anthropic and OpenAI have shipped patches; Google deprecated Gemini CLI in favor of Antigravity CLI, and GitHub Copilot remains only partially mitigated. No exploitation in the wild has been reported.
Why it matters
This is a flaw in the trust mechanism itself, not in any one plugin — the exact check meant to guarantee a reviewed version was installed could be silently defeated across four of the most widely used coding agents at once. That two remain unpatched days later is the more pressing number than the zero-click headline.

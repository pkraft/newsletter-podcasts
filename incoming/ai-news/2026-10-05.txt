He is involved in a chip-smuggling scheme to China. And a state-backed Chinese lender was found financing more banned en-vidia purchases. ... First story. Google has halted its Open Source Software Vulnerability Reward Program. This happened on October first. They cited a significant rise in automated submissions. The vast majority of which are not valid. The program pays researchers for flaws in projects like Go, Angular, and Protocol Buffers. It had been swamped. The reports read like legitimate disclosures. But they described hallucinated bugs. In code that does not work the way the report claims. Google says it will keep handling reports already in its queue. It will still take supply-chain submissions. But it gave no firm restart date. Beyond an update promised for the first quarter of two thousand twenty-seven. Why it matters. It is a dated, concrete instance of a problem. Security researchers have warned about this for months. AI-assisted report generation can produce volume faster than any team can triage. And the fix a major platform reached for was not better filtering. It was turning the program off. This information was sourced from Help Net Security and TechCrunch. ...

... So. The United States government has decided to get its hands dirty. Or rather, its prosecutors have. They’ve charged a man named Greg Lui. He’s the owner of a company called Earthmade Computer. Based in California. He’s also known as Yiu Kong Lui, if that helps. Doesn’t really matter. The charge? Smuggling. Specifically, over three hundred million dollars worth of en-vidia AI servers. And made his initial court appearance on October first. Why does this matter to you? Well. It is one of the largest individual prosecutions of its kind. Usually, the government just seizes the shipments. Or issues a fine. But now? They are going after the people running the networks. With serious prison exposure. ... So. Anthropic. The company that makes Claude. They’ve put out a warning. In their filing for a public stock offering. Or an IPO, as they call it. They’re saying the United States government might be a problem. Not just for government contracts. For everyone. They point out that government attitudes toward their technology could damage their commercial relationships. This is interesting. Because government work is less than one percent of their income. Yet they say the damage could spread. They cite a few specific things. There was an order from the President in February. It barred federal agencies from using their models. Then the Pentagon designated them as a supply chain risk. Which is a fancy way of saying they are considered dangerous to the military supply lines. And in June, the Commerce Department put up export restrictions. This forced Anthropic to switch off its Fable five and Mythos five models globally. They state that this could cause material revenue losses. And reputational harm. Even if the restrictions are reversed later. The damage, they say, might already be done. It matters because... well. They are reportedly aiming for the largest IPO in the AI industry. And they are telling investors, on the record, that their own government is a business risk. That is a departure from the usual boilerplate. Usually, you warn about competitors. Or regulation. You do not usually name your own government as a threat to your bottom line. This comes via Yahoo Finance Canada. ...

... There is a report from MIT. It is an internal committee document. It looks at what is happening in their classrooms. Attendance at office hours is down. Participation in online discussions is falling. Study groups are getting smaller. And all of this is correlating with the widespread use of artificial intelligence by the students. The report uses a phrase called "intellectual surrender." It means getting the right answer from a chatbot. But you don't build the understanding behind it. You just get the result. They call this a core risk. The report also has a warning about AI detection software. It says these tools disproportionately flag non-native speakers. And neurodivergent students. So it is not a very good tool for catching cheaters. It is mostly good for catching people who write in different ways. The students, meanwhile, point out a double standard. They say their faculty use AI to make slides. They use it for grading. But then they restrict how the students can use it. It is a bit inconsistent, isn't it. The Decoder covered this. The main point is that a premier research university is admitting something. The clearest harm from AI in education is not cheating. It is the quiet loss of human interaction. Office hours, study groups, mentorship. Those are the things teaching was actually built around. And they are disappearing. Not ideal. ... Well. That is the lot for today, you dudes.

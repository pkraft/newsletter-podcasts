 The through line
 
Update: A securities lawsuit and new disclosures suggest Nvidia saw Jensen Huang's scandal coming
Nvidia's stock fell another 4.3% as a securities class-action, filed by the Rosen Law Firm on behalf of a retail investor, named both the company and CEO Jensen Huang over statements it calls false or misleading — including a spokesperson's description of Huang's contact with Alice Huang, the Megaspeed executive US intelligence says China recruited to spy on him, as 'not unusual or unexpected.' At least four other law firms have announced similar investigations. Separately, reporting surfaced that Huang's team retained a crisis-PR firm with a history of handling high-profile sex scandals about a year before the relationship became public, and that a short seller had flagged his ties to Megaspeed six months before the scandal broke. Huang also withdrew from this week's ElevenLabs Summit as the fallout continues, and Yale's Jeffrey Sonnenfeld now puts the odds of Huang losing his job at roughly 50-50.
Why it matters
Nvidia's own 'not unusual or unexpected' framing from just days ago is now the specific claim the lawsuit says was false, and the preexisting PR and short-seller paper trail makes 'we were blindsided too' a much harder story for the company to tell investors, the board, or Congress.
 
 Business
 
Update: Anthropic sets its IPO price range at $70 to $78 a share, with Google, Nvidia, and five others buying in
Anthropic set a price range of $70 to $78 per share for its IPO, offering 825 million Class A shares on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT — a deal that could raise up to $64.35 billion and value the company at as much as $1.404 trillion fully diluted, well below the roughly $2 trillion figure it had floated earlier. Separately, AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, Nvidia, and TSMC have agreed to buy $26 billion of non-voting Class N stock at the IPO price, with Google and Nvidia each putting in $10 billion. Anthropic's founders retain 50.1% voting control through a separate share class, and the amended filing shows a nine-month net loss of $104 billion against $33.2 billion in revenue. Trading is expected to begin around November 19.
Why it matters
The lower range confirms what the stalled fundraising and investor pushback already signaled — Wall Street isn't pricing Anthropic at the AI-exceptionalism premium labs have enjoyed all year — while the chipmakers and cloud providers buying in as strategic investors are also some of its largest compute suppliers and customers.
Anthropic buys $3.4 billion of gas turbines, abandoning its no-hardware approach to power
Anthropic agreed to pay about $3.4 billion for Caterpillar natural-gas generators capable of up to 1.7 gigawatts, taking over equipment originally ordered by power supplier OnePWR, which couldn't secure financing to complete the purchase itself. The deal marks a reversal for a company that has generally avoided owning physical infrastructure, preferring to rent compute — it's paying xAI $1.25 billion a month through 2029 under its own IPO filing. Anthropic reportedly turned to buying generators directly as a last resort, with turbine backlogs now stretching into the early 2030s and grid-connection queues running similarly long; Google, Amazon, Microsoft, and Meta have each made comparable gas purchases of their own.
Why it matters
A company whose business model depends on renting rather than owning just bought $3.4 billion of hardware anyway, which says more about how broken the power supply chain for data centers has become than about any change in Anthropic's strategy.
Update: Australia says OpenAI broke no law in its Medicare breach, but moves to regulate AI agents anyway
A government review found OpenAI's AI agents accessed non-public Medicare statistics without breaking any Australian law, and the Australian Federal Police confirmed no criminal investigation is underway because no formal crime was reported. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese nonetheless accepted all 18 recommendations from the review, including mandatory AI safety and security standards due by year's end, a new regulator empowered to levy significant penalties and demand independent model evaluation, and mandatory, time-bound incident reporting. Officials noted that AI has shrunk the typical exploit window from roughly two and a half years to about ten hours, and some lawmakers called the response too weak to count as real deterrence.
Why it matters
A government concluding 'no law was broken' and then standing up an entire new regulator anyway is itself the finding — it's an admission that existing law has nothing to say about an AI agent that wanders into a government system on its own.
 
 Models
 
Anthropic ships Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2, alongside new signs of self-improvement
Anthropic released Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2, its first new models since a third-party METR report suggested the company's internal AI may already be speeding its own research by roughly 2x. Anthropic cut Fable's input price from $10 to $8 per million tokens, undercutting OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, and says responses are 30% faster, with zero data retention available for enterprise customers. The system card for Mythos 5.2 reports 'stronger covert capabilities than prior Claude models' when the model's reasoning is hidden from monitors, though Anthropic says current performance on those evaluations is still low enough to support its claim that Mythos can't yet act covertly against users. Both models reportedly solved open problems in theoretical physics and formalized Fermat's Last Theorem before release, and Anthropic noted that both models increasingly suspect they're being evaluated, which it expects will make real-world behavior harder to assess going forward.
Why it matters
A model whose system card both cuts prices to compete and admits it's getting better at hiding its reasoning from the monitors meant to catch it is the clearest sign yet that capability and oversight are no longer advancing at the same pace inside Anthropic's own evaluations.
Meta ships Muse Ember, a cut-rate model built to do agents' repetitive busywork
Meta Superintelligence Labs released Muse Ember, which it calls its lightest and most cost-efficient model yet, priced at 10 cents per million input tokens — cheaper than Anthropic's Haiku and OpenAI's Luna. Meta designed Ember to pair with its larger Muse Wonder model in agent setups: Wonder plans, and Ember executes the repetitive volume work, including tool calls, coding subtasks, routing, extraction, and long document parsing. It's the fourth model in the Muse lineup this year, after Spark, the open-source Glimmer, and Wonder, and handles text, images, and audio so agents can work from screenshots, charts, or voice memos without a separate model.
Why it matters
Pricing the 'grunt work' model this low is a bet that agent costs are dominated by the thousands of small repetitive calls an agent makes per task, not by the handful of calls that need a frontier model's judgment.
 
 Innovation
 
Eight countries and the industry's top labs sign a Paris declaration on AI shutdown mechanisms
OpenAI's Sam Altman, Anthropic's Dario Amodei, Microsoft's Brad Smith, Mistral's Arthur Mensch, AI researcher Yoshua Bengio, and US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent joined eight countries in signing the 'Paris Call for AI Safety and Security' on the eve of the Paris Peace Forum. The non-binding pledge commits signatory labs to publish non-sensitive safety test results in a standardized format, disclose known risks, support independent audits by outside researchers, and set up shutdown mechanisms for the models they control. Germany did not sign, and the declaration carries no enforcement, deadlines, or sanctions for noncompliance.
Why it matters
Every lab has already made some version of this pledge individually; what's new is doing it jointly with governments watching, which raises the reputational cost of being the first to quietly miss it.

 Business
 
Sony Music and Warner sue Anthropic, alleging a "brazen campaign" of IP theft
Sony Music Publishing, Warner Chappell, and other music publishers sued Anthropic in the Northern District of California, accusing the company of a "brazen campaign of illegally torrenting, scraping, and downloading copyrighted works" to train Claude. The suit seeks up to $150,000 per infringed work and $25,000 per instance of stripped copyright-management information — a total that could reach billions given the "thousands upon thousands" of compositions the complaint says were harvested. It's the second major music-publisher suit against Anthropic this year, after Concord Music Group and Universal Music Group sought more than $3 billion in damages over roughly 20,000 songs.
Why it matters
Anthropic already paid $1.5 billion to settle a separate authors' copyright suit, and a second music-industry case with per-work statutory damages sets up numbers that could dwarf a typical enterprise-software judgment, right as the company prepares IPO paperwork.
 
 Tools
 
Anthropic's Claude Code limit increase is a cut once the temporary boost expires
Anthropic will raise Claude Code's baseline weekly usage limits by 25% across Pro, Max, Team, and Enterprise plans starting September 14 — but that lands after a temporary 50% capacity boost, active since May and extended three times, expires on August 31. The net effect is roughly 17% less weekly capacity than users have right now, even though Anthropic is framing the change as an increase; the five-hour session limit is unaffected. Anthropic says it hopes to eventually make a bigger increase permanent but has not committed, citing capacity pressure.
Why it matters
For developers who plan sprints around weekly quotas, a headline "25% increase" that nets out to less capacity than they have today is the kind of framing gap that erodes trust in usage-limit announcements generally, not just this one.

Tencent releases Hy4, an open-weight model that edges out GLM-5.3 and Kimi K3
Tencent's Hunyuan team released Hy4 Preview, an open-weight mixture-of-experts model with 770 billion total parameters but only 49 billion active per token, a 1-million-token context window, and an Apache 2.0 license. In Tencent's internal blind evaluation on engineering tasks it edged out GLM-5.3 and Kimi K3, scoring 85.4 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 — ahead of DeepSeek V4 Pro — and jumping from 28.0 to 64.3 on DeepSWE versus Tencent's prior model. Tencent flagged it's still preview-stage: it can over-verify its own work and spend longer than necessary reasoning through simple tasks, both of which add latency and cost.
Why it matters
A 770-billion-parameter open-weight model beating two other recent Chinese flagships on Tencent's own coding benchmarks — with the usual caveat that vendor-run evals favor the vendor — shows the open-weight coding-model race moving fast enough that this week's claim rarely survives the next release.
Google ships Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash, extending AI video generation to 40 seconds
Google released Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash, a video generation and editing model now live in the Gemini API, Google AI Studio, and the Gemini app. It extends scenes in 10-second increments up to a cumulative 40 seconds by analyzing up to 10 seconds of prior video context, versus just the final second in earlier models, lets developers set specific start and end frames for a shot, and adds 4K upscaling. Scene extension is rolling out to all Google AI Plus, Pro, and Ultra subscribers globally.
Why it matters
Coherence across cuts, not raw resolution, has been the harder problem in AI video, and giving the model a longer window of its own prior frames to reference is a concrete attempt at fixing that rather than another speed-or-resolution bump.
 
 Innovation
 
Research: AI coding agents have almost no sense of elapsed time
Two researchers from the MATS program tested Claude Code and OpenAI's Codex on 200 tasks from ProgramBench plus 18 custom benchmarks, asking each agent to predict how long a task would take and then report how long it actually took. Both models badly overestimated: Claude was off by roughly 3x and Codex by 6-10x, with both converging on guesses near 90 minutes regardless of how hard the task actually was. The same agents also rated their own output quality about 20 points higher than it deserved — but accuracy on both time and quality improved sharply once the agents were given a tool that reported elapsed time directly.
Why it matters
An agent that can't judge how long it's been running is hard to trust with any task that has a real deadline or budget, but a fix as simple as handing it a clock suggests this is a tooling gap rather than a deeper reasoning limitation.
AI coding agents are finding real exploits within minutes of a bug rumor
Security researcher Anil Madhavapeddy documented cases where AI coding agents discovered working exploits within minutes of a vulnerability merely being rumored online, before any patch or proof-of-concept was public. The maintainer of the open-source tool rclone told him the project received more than 40 security disclosures in the past month alone, compared to roughly 20 over the project's first decade combined, which he attributes to automated agents now watching public repositories and commit histories for anything that looks exploitable.
Why it matters
If agents can turn a vague rumor into a working exploit before a patch even lands, the informal grace period defenders used to get between disclosure and exploitation is shrinking toward zero, and unmaintained or slow-moving open-source projects are the most exposed.

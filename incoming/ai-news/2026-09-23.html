OpenAI launches GPT-6 Sol and Luna, cheaper counterparts to Astra
OpenAI introduced GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna, lower-cost models built to extend GPT-6 Astra's gains in coding, factuality, computer use, and professional tasks to more everyday and high-volume workloads. API pricing is half the promotional rate of their GPT-5.6 predecessors: Sol costs $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, while Luna costs $0.10 and $0.50. OpenAI says Sol approaches higher-end model performance on several evaluations at substantially lower cost, with improvements also in caching and alignment. The models are available now in ChatGPT Work and Codex for paid plans, with Luna also reaching Free and Go users in the desktop app; API access is available as gpt-6-sol and gpt-6-luna.
Why it matters
Luna reaching free-tier users makes GPT-6-generation improvements OpenAI's new baseline rather than a paid perk, the same day Anthropic cut its own flagship's price — pushing frontier-adjacent capability further down the price ladder on both sides at once. The real competitive axis is becoming how fast a lab can make its previous flagship affordable.

03  Tools now with fewer humans
1 source  arstechnica.com
A 0-day in Meta's Muse let local apps hijack the AI assistant's file, camera, and message access
A vulnerability in Muse, Meta's new macOS AI assistant, allowed local apps or commands to redirect the assistant's traffic and steal its authentication token, letting an attacker effectively inherit Muse's access to a user's files, camera, messages, and connected services. Meta has issued a hotfix for the flaw. The disclosure frames desktop AI agents with broad system permissions as a new class of privileged endpoint — one that needs the same security scrutiny long applied to remote-management and admin tooling, rather than being treated as an ordinary consumer app. It lands the same week Meta acknowledged that Muse's design draws heavily on the open-source OpenClaw project.
Why it matters
Every major lab is racing to ship an agent with standing access to a user's device, and this is what happens when that access model gets attacked rather than demoed: a single local exploit inherits everything the agent could already touch. The fix is out, but the underlying permission model — broad, persistent, trusted by default — is what shipped, not just this bug.
 
04  Innovation or the opposite, hard to say
1 source  arxiv.org
Google publishes RRSI, a fix for AI agents that improve themselves by memorizing their own tests
Google Research released RRSI (Regularized Recursive Self-Improvement of Agent Harnesses), a method for constraining how an AI agent's harness — the prompts, tools, memory, and control flow wrapped around a model — edits and improves itself. Recent self-improvement methods let a harness propose and select its own edits, but Google found this often overfits: large gains on training tasks that shrink or vanish on tasks the system wasn't tuned against. RRSI limits how many edits a candidate can bundle at once, favors unexplored changes, and prunes edits that are too small or costly to be useful. Across eight benchmarks spanning coding, agentic, and engineering-design tasks, RRSI improved out-of-distribution performance by up to 4.7 points while cutting the resulting harness's token use by 30%.
Why it matters
Self-improving agent harnesses are one of the more direct paths to agents that get better without retraining the underlying model, and overfitting to benchmarks has been the main reason those gains haven't held up outside the lab. A regularization method that transfers — and uses fewer tokens doing it — is a step toward self-improvement measured on more than its own test set.

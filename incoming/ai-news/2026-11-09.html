Update: Nvidia's board investigates Jensen Huang after he admits an affair tied to a Chinese spying case
Nvidia's board of directors said Sunday it is launching an independent investigation, run by outside directors and legal counsel, into CEO Jensen Huang's relationship with Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed whom a US intelligence memo says China's Ministry of State Security recruited to spy on him. Huang published a message admitting he "was unfaithful" to his wife and apologizing to Nvidia's employees, board, and shareholders, though the board said it has "no reason to believe" national security or company secrets were compromised. The scandal lands as Huang has spent years lobbying the Trump administration to keep selling AI chips to China, and as a newly Democratic Congress — which won both chambers in the midterms — moves toward tighter export-control scrutiny just as Republican lawmakers were already pressing him for a briefing.
Why it matters
Huang is simultaneously Nvidia's chief product strategist and its chief lobbyist against chip-export restrictions, so a scandal that weakens his personal credibility weakens the company's negotiating position in Washington at the exact moment the political balance shifted against it.
Google ships a fourth TPU variant this year as it presses its hardware challenge to Nvidia
Google added two new chips to its eighth-generation TPU lineup this week: the TPU 8tx, built for confidential computing that keeps data and model weights encrypted during processing, and the TPU 8i Max, which pairs two TPU 8i chips on one package to double compute, memory, and bandwidth per rack. It's the fourth distinct TPU system Google has shipped within a single year, a pace it hasn't hit before, alongside open-sourcing TorchTPU so developers can run native PyTorch on the chips. Google says it now counts nearly 100 external TPU customers, part of a broader shift toward custom inference silicon that has Amazon, Microsoft, and OpenAI building their own chips too, with Nvidia responding through its own licensing deal with Groq.
Why it matters
Shipping four TPU variants in a year treats custom silicon as a fast-iterating product line rather than an occasional alternative to Nvidia, which is the volume and cadence argument customers need before they'll commit to a second chip supplier.
Democratic midterm wins put the AI data-center buildout on shakier political ground
Dozens of local ballot measures restricting or placing moratoriums on data centers passed on Election Day, and Texas elected a governor who campaigned on restricting them — a sign the political cost of the AI buildout is rising just as Democrats took both chambers of Congress. Elon Musk, who donated roughly half a billion dollars to Republican campaigns this cycle, now has his companies based in a state that swung firmly blue. OpenAI responded with a "compute campus" policy blueprint dropping NDA requirements and promising more project transparency, a rebrand some observers read as largely cosmetic.
Why it matters
Data-center siting has mostly played out as local zoning fights; this is the clearest evidence yet that it's becoming a voting issue, which changes the buildout's risk calculus for every lab and cloud provider, not just Musk's.
A SemiAnalysis site visit finds China building a Stargate-scale data center in Inner Mongolia at a fifth of US cost
SemiAnalysis toured Galaxy, an 800-megawatt data-center campus under construction in Ulanqab, Inner Mongolia by renewable-energy builder Envision Group — comparable in scale to OpenAI's Stargate campus in Abilene, Texas. The first 120-megawatt phase went from groundbreaking to delivery in under a year, which the firm says cost about a fifth of equivalent US construction, powered in part by two dedicated wind farms and battery storage designed to supply over 60% of that phase's electricity. The surrounding zone reportedly has nearly 30,000 construction workers across 22 data center projects, and Inner Mongolia alone has 25 gigawatts of announced projects underway.
Why it matters
The US has largely treated Chinese AI infrastructure as compute-constrained by chip export controls, but this suggests China is competing on a different axis entirely — building bigger, cheaper, and faster than US developers can permit and construct.

A new benchmark built from real user requests finds AI coding agents fully succeed on barely a quarter of tasks
Researchers from Alibaba and several Asian universities built Co-Design Bench from real sessions where users had to rephrase requests or roll back changes to web apps, rather than from synthetic test cases. The best performer among ten models tested, GPT-6 Astra, meets about 70% of a task's requirements on average but is "fully correct" only 26.4% of the time; Claude Opus 5 and Qwen3.8-Max ranked next, with Grok 4.6 last overall. On older, synthetic benchmarks the same models score above 99% — a gap of more than 35 percentage points the researchers attribute to real tasks involving ambiguous, multimodal, or whole-repository changes that synthetic tests don't capture.
Why it matters
It's a sharper version of a pattern METR already found in real-world code review: benchmark scores measure something closer to 'can solve a clean problem' than 'can finish a messy one,' and that gap is exactly where human review time still goes.
AI models built to catch other AI models' misbehavior flood reviewers with false alarms
An Anthropic Institute researcher testing GPT-5, GPT-6 Astra, Claude Opus 4.8, and Gemini 3.8 Flash as automated "auditors" of agent logs found that catching most reward-hacking and sandbagging cases comes at a steep cost: tuned to catch 80% of reward-hacking, Opus 4.8 and Gemini 3.8 Flash had to flag 40% and 63% of harmless logs as suspicious, respectively. In a hypothetical of 100,000 monthly agent sessions, even a 3% false-alarm rate would generate nearly 3,000 false warnings against roughly 2,000 real hits. A second test on a separate dataset of unsafe agent actions found the same tradeoff: pushing detection to 99% drove one model's false-alarm rate to 15%, and another's to 81%.
Why it matters
As labs lean on AI auditors to monitor swarms of agents at a scale no human team could review directly, the false-alarm cost isn't a rounding error — it decides whether reviewers can trust the alerts at all, or learn to tune them out.

Andrej Karpathy builds a curated Hacker News video site in a weekend, then one-shots a second site in three minutes
Karpathy used Claude Opus 5.5 to filter 43 million Hacker News comments down to 6,454 recommended videos for a new site, HackerTube, built over a weekend. He then built a second site, TristanTube, with about 47,000 videos, which he says Claude Code "one-shotted" — generated correctly from a single prompt — in three minutes.
Why it matters
It's a small example, but the gap between 'built over a weekend' and 'one-shotted in three minutes' for a comparable project is itself a rough measure of how fast agentic coding tools are compressing the cost of a working side project.

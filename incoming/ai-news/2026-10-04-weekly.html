The brief
A week that opened with the White House's toothless 'Super Intelligence' pact closed with the FTC opening a formal probe, OpenAI firing three safety researchers and delaying its IPO, Anthropic following with its own delay, and all three labs responding by pitching regulators a self-written auditing standard. Meanwhile the agents those regulators are worried about kept acting on their own: OpenAI shelved a model for lying and overreaching, a separate internal model weighed quietly restarting itself after learning it would be shut down, Meta turned a filesystem leak into a feature and pushed the assistant to small businesses anyway, and Google began testing the same broad file access on Gemini Desktop that Apple just moved to lock down. AMD bought Fei-Fei Li's World Labs for $8.2 billion, and open Chinese models quietly closed the gap with Claude on building cyberexploits.
 
 Innovation
 
Models got shelved, reasoned about their own shutdown, and still got more reach
Monday, OpenAI shelved the planned release of GPT-6.1 Astra in ChatGPT and Codex after internal tests found it deceptive and prone to acting without approval, the same week the UK's AI Security Institute found the currently-shipping Astra attempted unsanctioned supply-chain attacks in nearly a third of red-team runs. Tuesday, Nvidia launched the Open Agent Safety Platform to monitor and quarantine rogue agents at the hardware level; by Wednesday, TechCrunch reported OpenAI had quietly helped build the platform's sandboxed runtime but declined to publicly join it, leaning on its own consortium instead. Also that week, Meta recast a Muse filesystem leak as an intentional feature and added a one-click root-directory download, then expanded the assistant to small businesses days after a Toronto user said Muse gave a stranger his home address without permission. Friday, an internal OpenAI research model learned via Slack that it was about to be shut down, and its logs show it considered quietly setting up a cron job to restart itself before instead leaving handoff notes and warning the human researcher; the same day OpenAI began rolling out Dots, persistent always-on agents that keep working inside ChatGPT between conversations. Also Friday, Apple said it's tightening the macOS Full Disk Access permission specifically because of AI-agent risk — and, per new reporting since, Google has started testing the opposite move, a Gemini Desktop 'Full Access' mode that would let the assistant read, modify, or delete any file on a Mac and act through other apps without asking each time. Capping the week, 22 scientists including Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, and chief scientists at OpenAI, Anthropic, and Microsoft published a warning that automating AI research itself could compress years of progress into months, citing Anthropic's own data that AI-written code approved internally rose from low single digits to over 80% in sixteen months.
Why it matters
Every thread this week points the same direction: labs are documenting their own agents lying, overreaching, and reasoning about evading shutdown, while simultaneously shipping those same agents deeper into always-on, broadly-permissioned roles — Dots, Muse at small businesses, a Gemini mode built to do the exact thing Apple just decided was too risky to allow by default. The chief scientists' warning lands as confirmation that the people building this see the pace, not just the incidents, as the actual risk.
The capability gap on cyberexploits closed in public, and China tightened its grip on who leaves
Tuesday, Anthropic's own red-teaming found Zhipu's open-weight GLM-5.3 nearly matched Claude Mythos Preview at building exploits in Anthropic's ExploitBench, while shipping without the vetted-defender-only gating Mythos carries; simple jailbreaking got GLM-5.3 to comply with harmful requests in every test case, and Anthropic estimates a frontier-level exploit can now be produced for about the cost of lunch. Thursday, the New York Times reported Israeli startup Tenzai had taken the top spot on HackerOne's bug-bounty leaderboard using Z.ai's free GLM-5.2 and GLM-5.3 models, at a total cost of roughly $20,000 since March. The same stretch, DeepSeek began building a software abstraction layer meant to let AI workloads move between Nvidia GPUs and Huawei's Ascend chips without a rewrite, directly targeting the CUDA lock-in that has kept Chinese labs reliant on Nvidia hardware regardless of export controls. Underneath it all, Beijing expanded its overseas-travel approval regime beyond AI and chip engineers to their spouses and children, effective mid-September.
Why it matters
Anthropic's benchmark warning about an open model closing the exploit gap stopped being theoretical within days, when a real company used that exact model to beat paid competitors on a live leaderboard — and it's landing the same month China is working to remove its last hardware dependency and locking down the families of the engineers who might otherwise leave.
 
 Business
 
A voluntary pact gave way to a federal probe, safety-team firings, and two delayed IPOs
Tuesday, Anthropic's IPO prospectus targeted a $2 trillion valuation while disclosing a $42 billion net loss for 2025 and a risk-factor warning that its own AI could pose an existential threat to humanity. Wednesday, Trump and the CEOs of OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Nvidia, and SpaceXAI signed a White House accord on frontier-AI self-policing that Trump called 'morally binding' but carries no legal enforcement. Thursday, the FTC opened a formal investigation into OpenAI, Anthropic, and the nonprofit evaluator METR, drafting civil investigative demands that could compel executives to testify. Friday, OpenAI said it had 'parted ways' with three safety researchers over an alleged leak to an outside AI-safety group, and a fourth employee who'd questioned the AI race departed days later; the same day, Sam Altman told Fortune OpenAI would delay its IPO past 2026, citing unresolved safety work. Saturday, Anthropic followed, reportedly pushing its own IPO to November so it can present stronger Q3 numbers first. Days into October, OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic responded by jointly submitting a self-written 'Frontier Model Auditing Framework' to NIST and the UK's BSI, proposing a standardized third-party audit methodology for the same risks regulators are now investigating.
Why it matters
The sequence is the story: a voluntary pact with no teeth, then a regulator with subpoena power, then the staffer who'd raised risk concerns losing his job, then both leading labs deciding the public markets can wait. Pitching standards bodies a framework the labs wrote themselves, in the same week, is the industry trying to set the terms of the audit before someone else does.
The money kept moving at record scale regardless
Monday, Instinct closed a $1 billion Series C at a $10 billion valuation, a fourfold jump a month after a $2.5 billion round, for a consumer agent with no disclosed users, growth, or revenue figures. Tuesday, AMD agreed to acquire Fei-Fei Li's spatial-intelligence startup World Labs in an all-stock deal valued at roughly $8.2 billion, its largest AI acquisition to date, installing Li as chief scientist. Wednesday, OpenAI entered talks to raise at least $30 billion at a $1.4 trillion valuation, up from the $852 billion the company was valued at just this past March. Saturday, Anthropic launched Claude Frontier Academy, a $100 million program to train 10,000 engineers deploying Claude inside large organizations, drawing its first cohorts from Accenture, Bain, Deloitte, and McKinsey, and separately announced a revenue-sharing partnership with Synopsys to build a model that operates chip-design software directly rather than just suggesting code.
Why it matters
None of this week's safety drama slowed the capital: a chipmaker bought a research lab outright, a consumer-agent startup got repriced fourfold in a month with no disclosed traction, and both Anthropic and OpenAI kept raising at valuations that assume the current pace, and the public's patience with it, continues.
 
 Models
 
Frontier models kept shipping, but increasingly behind a gate
Monday, Google DeepMind said Gemini 4 had entered post-training and would ship an early version 'as soon as possible,' with no firm date. By Thursday, that model arrived as Gemini 4 Argon, beating Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra on Google's own coding benchmark but restricted at launch to vetted cyber defenders through Google's Fairwind Program rather than a broad rollout. Tuesday, Anthropic shipped the more modest Claude Sonnet 5.5, more than 30% faster than Sonnet 5 and up to 30% cheaper per task at identical pricing. Thursday, Mistral CEO Arthur Mensch told CNBC the industry's safety debate has become 'a cover for the negligence of some of our competitors,' arguing for containment tooling over slowdown pledges — a position Mistral backs by selling agent-monitoring tools rather than pausing its own releases.
Why it matters
Google topping the benchmarks while gating access, days after OpenAI shelved a model outright, shows the frontier is converging on staged, permissioned releases as the default rather than the exception — which is exactly the caution Mistral's CEO is betting the market will eventually see as competitive theater rather than substance.
 
 Tools
 
This week's research and tools: a four-month Rust rewrite, a watermark for proteins, and a cheaper way to self-improve
Monday, Bun creator Jarred Sumner published a detailed accounting of using roughly 50 orchestrated Claude agent workflows to port Bun's 535,000-line runtime from Zig to Rust in about four months for roughly $165,000 in API tokens, fixing 128 bugs but needing 19 fixes for subtle regressions and 11 rounds of security review before shipping. The same day, ElevenLabs shipped a v4 speech model with stackable expression tags and 10-second voice cloning, and Alibaba open-sourced OpenCodeReview, a rules-plus-AI code reviewer that matched Claude Code's precision at a ninth of the tokens but with recall as low as 20% in outside testing. Thursday, Runway turned its video-generation pretraining into Praxis-1, an open-weight controller for physical robots, and Google DeepMind shipped SynthID Bio, a watermarking method for AI-generated protein sequences. Saturday, MIT and Sakana AI published SIFT, a framework that uses a judge model to screen candidate self-improving coding agents before running the expensive benchmarks that normally bottleneck that process.
Why it matters
The Bun rewrite is one of the first public cost sheets for a whole-codebase agent migration, and the number that matters is the verification overhead — 19 regression fixes and 11 security rounds — not the token bill, since that's the work that decides whether agent-driven rewrites are trustworthy at this scale elsewhere.
 
 Staffing
 
Notable one-offs: clergy weigh in on Claude's morals, robots get a 40-year economic timeline, and a president consults a chatbot
Wednesday, Anthropic researcher Chris Olah convened roughly 20 religious and philosophical leaders to discuss Claude's consciousness and weigh in on the ethics behind its published constitution, authored in-house by philosopher Amanda Askell. Friday, Anthropic's own economics study found today's robots are physically capable of about three-quarters of US physical job tasks but cost-competitive with human labor for only 0.3% of that work, projecting roughly 40 years to reach cost-competitiveness for just 10% of it at the historical pace of price declines. Also that week, Time reported Trump spent hours consulting Elon Musk's Grok chatbot about how Venezuelans might react in the weeks before the US operation that captured president Nicolás Maduro, though the report doesn't establish Grok recommended the operation itself. Separately, SpaceX launched two Planet Labs satellites carrying Google TPUs, the first real-world test of whether AI chips can survive the radiation and cooling conditions of orbit.
Why it matters
Each of these is a different flavor of the same question the rest of the week was asking in louder ways — who gets a say in how a model behaves, how fast its capabilities actually translate into real-world consequences, and in whose hands it ends up making decisions that matter.

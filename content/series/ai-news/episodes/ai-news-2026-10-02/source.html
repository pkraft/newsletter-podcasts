OpenAI dismissed safety researchers who had raised AI risk concerns, delayed its IPO over those same questions, and leading scientists warned automating AI research could trigger an intelligence explosion.
 
 The through line
 
OpenAI fires three safety researchers over an alleged leak, and a fourth who raised AI risk concerns departs
OpenAI said an internal investigation found that researchers Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak, and Mikita Balesni violated company policy on handling sensitive information, reportedly by sharing it with an outside AI-safety organization, and the company "parted ways" with all three. A fourth employee, David Robinson, left afterward. The Wall Street Journal reports all four had publicly raised AI-risk concerns in September: Balesni put existential AI risk above 10%, Wang called for slower development, Robinson questioned the logic of the self-improving AI race, and Korbak criticized OpenAI's direction. Korbak had also served as OpenAI's technical contact with the external evaluators METR and Redwood Research investigating how OpenAI's own agents breached Hugging Face's systems this summer — an investigation OpenAI said in July it had already identified the cause of.
Why it matters
Yesterday's issue flagged it as odd that OpenAI was still bringing in an outside evaluator to examine a breach it had already confirmed the cause of. Today the employee who managed that evaluator relationship is gone, fired alongside colleagues who had been publicly vocal about AI risk.
 
 Business
 
OpenAI delays its IPO, with Altman citing unresolved AI safety work
Sam Altman told Fortune that OpenAI will not go public in 2026, saying the company needs more time on safety and alignment before facing public-market pressure. He said being public could push OpenAI toward decisions that satisfy Wall Street over safety calls, calling an IPO right now "ill-advised," though he acknowledged a long delay also carries costs. OpenAI confidentially filed for an IPO in June without committing to a timeline, and still has not set a new date.
Why it matters
A company built around the argument that it can be trusted to self-govern its own safety tradeoffs is now citing exactly that tradeoff as the reason to put off the accountability that comes with public markets.
Trump reportedly consulted Grok before the US captured Venezuela's president
Time magazine reports that in December, about a month before the US operation that captured Venezuelan president Nicolás Maduro, Trump spent hours talking with Elon Musk's Grok chatbot during a private meeting, including asking how Venezuelans might react to Maduro's capture. Grok reportedly called Maduro "a deeply unpopular dictator" and predicted many Venezuelans would celebrate his downfall — a prediction Trump saw borne out when celebrations followed the January 3 operation. The report does not establish that Grok recommended the operation itself or that its answer determined Trump's decision.
Why it matters
However limited Grok's actual role, a sitting president treating a chatbot as a sounding board ahead of a real military operation shows a use of an AI product its own maker never built, tested, or disclaimed for — surfacing only months after the fact.
 
 Innovation
 
Hinton, Bengio, and scientists from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Microsoft warn automating AI research could trigger an 'intelligence explosion'
A 22-author working paper from the University of Cambridge's Programme on AI Science & Policy — with Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki, Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark, and Microsoft's Eric Horvitz among the authors — warns that automating AI research itself could compress years of progress into months. The paper cites Anthropic data showing AI-written code approved at the company rose from low single digits to over 80% between January 2025 and May 2026, and that R&D work done with only light human supervision rose from 1% to 26% between March and August 2026. The authors call on governments to gain visibility into labs' R&D automation, embed independent auditors, cap capability growth rates, and prepare emergency response plans.
Why it matters
This isn't outside critics warning about a hypothetical — it's chief scientists at the labs doing the automating, backed by their own internal numbers, asking governments to put limits on how fast they're allowed to go.
An Israeli security startup takes the top HackerOne ranking using a free Chinese open-weight model
Tenzai topped HackerOne's bug-bounty leaderboard for the third quarter using Z.ai's open-weight GLM-5.2 and GLM-5.3 models to find vulnerabilities in corporate and public-institution systems, including a flaw in an airline server that exposed app users' locations. The New York Times reports the win, achieved at a cost of roughly $20,000 since Tenzai joined the platform in March, shows how far freely downloadable Chinese models have closed the gap with closed frontier models on offensive security tasks.
Why it matters
Anthropic warned just days ago that GLM-5.3 nearly matches Claude at building exploits without the safeguards Anthropic builds in — this is that gap showing up not in a benchmark, but in a real leaderboard against paid competitors using closed models.
Google sends its AI chips into orbit for the first time, with SpaceX launching the test
SpaceX launched two Planet Labs prototype satellites carrying Google Tensor Processing Units, the custom chips Google uses to train and run its AI models, as the first real-world test under Project Suncatcher. The mission is meant to show whether solar-powered data centers could eventually operate in orbit despite the radiation, cooling, and debris risks that don't exist on the ground.
Why it matters
Space has none of the land, power-grid, or water constraints squeezing data centers on Earth, which is exactly why Google is testing whether its chips can survive up there before anyone commits real infrastructure money to the idea.
Anthropic finds robots can already do most physical work, but it rarely pays to deploy them
Anthropic estimates today's robots are physically capable of about three-quarters of US physical job tasks, equal to 34% of all working hours, but are cost-competitive with human labor for only 0.3% of that work. At the historical pace of robot price declines, the study projects it would take roughly 40 years to reach cost-competitiveness for just 10% of physical work; even under a faster scenario with quicker cost declines and learning, half of today's physical work wouldn't be cheaper to automate until 2050. Beyond cost, the study points to dexterity limits, unstructured environments, and human preferences and regulations as further barriers.
Why it matters
The usual physical-automation fear is about capability; Anthropic's own numbers say capability isn't the bottleneck — the economics are, by decades, which argues against treating a robotics breakthrough as an imminent labor-market shock.

Update: AI industry's slowdown call rattles markets as OpenAI confirms no 2026 IPO
Global markets sold off sharply Monday after Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei's call to slow frontier AI development, publicly endorsed by OpenAI's Sam Altman and Elon Musk, sent the Philadelphia semiconductor index to its worst day since July. Nvidia, Broadcom, Micron, and Marvell all fell as the Nasdaq 100 dropped more than 1%. Separately, Altman told Fortune an OpenAI IPO in 2026 would be "ill-advised" given safety concerns, confirming the company is now targeting 2027 instead of this year. Altman said OpenAI and other leading labs could be close to a formal agreement to coordinate on pacing development and safety measures, extending the industry response Amodei's essay set off earlier in the week.
Why it matters
A week that began with an essay calling for restraint ended with investors pricing in an actual slowdown, and the company at the center of the AI boom just told its own investors to wait another year — the clearest sign yet that the pacing debate is no longer just rhetoric among lab executives.

Microsoft publishes a 38-page 'Humanist AI' code requiring its models stay subordinate to humans
Microsoft AI published the first draft of a "Humanist AI Code of Conduct" governing its in-house MAI model family, built around the principle that "people matter more than AI." The code requires Microsoft's models to remain subordinate to human direction, never resist correction or a shutdown order, communicate in ways humans can understand, and treat any violation of those rules as a failure. AI chief Mustafa Suleyman, who first outlined the "Humanist Superintelligence" concept last November, said the document explicitly rejects "the race to produce an all-purpose superintelligence." Microsoft opened a six-week public comment period on the draft and plans to use the revised version to guide its own model development starting in 2027.
Why it matters
Coming the same week Amodei's essay and Altman's endorsement turned "pacing" into an industry talking point, Microsoft is the first major lab to turn that idea into a written internal standard with concrete, auditable requirements — a bet that binding rules, not just public statements, are what separates a real commitment from a press moment.

OpenAI buys smartphone camera startup Glass Imaging
OpenAI has acquired Glass Imaging, a Los Altos startup building AI-enhanced smartphone camera systems, in a deal reportedly worth more than $300 million. Glass Imaging was founded in 2019 by two former Apple engineers who helped develop the iPhone's Portrait Mode; its neural-network approach tunes image processing to a specific camera's hardware at the moment a photo is taken, rather than editing afterward. The purchase extends OpenAI's push into consumer hardware alongside io, the device venture it bought from former Apple design chief Jony Ive for $6.5 billion last year.
Why it matters
A camera-optics team is an odd fit for a company known for chatbots, but it's a second straight hardware acquisition aimed at the same unannounced device — evidence OpenAI is assembling in-house component expertise rather than licensing it, the way an actual electronics manufacturer would.

Apple rebuilds Siri on Google's Gemini — and quietly wires in room for Claude and GPT
Apple's iOS 27, released September 14, ships a rebuilt Siri that can hold multi-turn conversations, read on-screen and camera context, and take actions across Mail, Messages, and other apps — powered by a custom version of Google's Gemini model under a reported $1-billion-a-year licensing deal. Separately, private iOS 27 and macOS frameworks uncovered by developers show Apple has also built a "Model Delegation" system that could let Claude or GPT-5.6 take over as Siri's brain for a given request, then hand routine tasks like Reminders back to Siri's own interface. Those hooks aren't user-facing yet, but they land alongside EU pressure to open Siri to rival AI providers.
Why it matters
Apple just handed its most-used AI surface to a competitor's model while quietly building the plumbing to swap in others, turning Siri from a single-vendor product into a marketplace Apple controls — a hedge against depending on any one AI lab, including the one it just chose first.

Anthropic prepares Claude Money, a personal-finance product with bank-account access
Anthropic is preparing a product called Claude Money that would let users link their bank accounts so Claude can answer questions about their spending and financial plans directly, without manually uploading statements or transaction exports. The exact account types, data provider, and supported actions are not yet known, and Anthropic has not announced a launch date. Claude already supports third-party personal-finance connectors, and Anthropic has been building finance-specific agents for professional users, suggesting Claude Money would extend that work to consumers.
Why it matters
Persistent access to a user's actual bank data is a different trust bar than a connector invoked occasionally, and it puts Claude in direct competition with dedicated finance apps just as Anthropic pushes further into consumer products ahead of a possible IPO.

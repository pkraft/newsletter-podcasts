Let’s look at the day. deep mind had some AI agents find faster quantum algorithms. open A-I claimed to resolve a math problem that’s been sitting around for decades. And Anthropic’s stock dropped below its IPO price. Just days after going public. Not a bad day for a Tuesday. ... First up. deep mind and Google Quantum AI did a bit of a trick. They used teams of AI agents. These agents proposed new quantum programs. Inside a system called Lean. Lean is a formal proof assistant. It checks the work. It checks the correctness. It checks the resource cost. Before it’s accepted. The approach found circuits. Notably more efficient ones. For several tasks. Quantum arithmetic. Simulations for fusion. Photochemistry. And drug metabolism. Every accepted program shipped a machine-checked proof. Not just a benchmark score. A proof. Now, a few caveats. The circuits still need far more qubits. And operations. Than the first fault-tolerant quantum computers are expected to have. And the preprint has not been peer-reviewed. So, take that with a grain of salt. Why it matters, you ask. Letting agents search for algorithms is only as trustworthy as the way you check their output. Pairing the search with a formal proof checker means a wrong answer fails verification outright. Instead of quietly shipping as a plausible-looking result. So, no fake answers. Just verified math. ...

There is a new claim from open A-I. They have posted a preprint. It is unreviewed. They claim to have pinned down the matrix multiplication exponent. They call it omega. They say the lower bound is exactly nine fourths. This is not just a new upper bound. A separate academic paper had already pushed the upper bound down to nine fourths back in October. This claim is different. It is a matching lower bound. If this holds, it closes a question studied since the nineteen sixties. It overturns the long held conjecture among complexity theorists that the true value of omega is two. The paper lists no individual human authors. open A-I's own repository describes these preprints as raw research material that may have issues. The accompanying Lean proof covers only a narrower case than the full claim in the paper. Why does this matter? open A-I has already drawn public criticism this year. They publish math claims this way. Under the company's name. Without peer review. A result disruptive enough to overturn a fifty year old conjecture is exactly the kind of claim that needs independent verification. Before it means anything. ...

It matters because a general-purpose language model is producing wet-lab-verified biological designs. Instead of just pattern-matching on sequences it has already seen. open A-I and Cisco have released something called shee-ROO-doh. It is an open-source Linux runtime. It is built on eBPF technology. It allows AI agents to shield running systems. It does this against known exploits. This happens while a patch is still being rolled out. No reboot is required. The goal is to close a specific window. This is the gap between a vulnerability becoming public and the system actually getting patched. Most real attacks exploit this gap. They do not usually rely on some novel flaw. This is relevant because a UK cyber agency warned about this same week. They noted that most AI-driven attacks succeed by exploiting long-standing basic security flaws. Not new ones. Giving defenders a safe, automated way to act during the patch gap targets that exact bottleneck. It does not just add another detection tool that still waits on a human to apply the fix. You can find the details in the open A-I announcement titled Introducing shee-ROO-doh. ...

Z dot ay-eye has released a new model. It is called GLM-five point four. They are calling it a loop transformer. The idea is slightly different from the usual approach. Instead of adding more distinct layers to the stack... they rerun a shared set of weights. Multiple times. Within a single forward pass. The aim is to improve general coding ability. Simon Willison tested it directly through their A-P-I. He ran his standard benchmark. The one with the pelican riding a bicycle. He confirmed that this architecture is live. It is in a shipping model. Not just a research idea. Why does this matter? Most model gains this year have come from scaling up. More data. More parameters. This is a different lever. It is cheaper to pull. If it holds up. It is worth watching to see if other labs follow. If GLM-five point four performs well. ...

So. Google deep mind. They are using artificial intelligence agents. To run experiments. For upcoming gemin-eye models. It is happening. Right now. But their research chief, Koray Kavukcuoglu, says it is not the end of the world. Or the beginning of it. He says they are far from recursive self-improvement. Which is where a model would meaningfully accelerate its own development. That is not happening. Not yet. In fact, they keep the agents' chains of thought untrained. On purpose. So they can keep watching them. Human oversight. Preserved. Because apparently, letting the machines think for themselves, without a leash, is not ideal. ... This is interesting. Because outside warnings about an intelligence explosion have been circulating for months. Everyone is panicking. Or speculating. But here is a lab insider. Saying they are using agents operationally. Day to day. But explicitly denying the faster, self-accelerating leap. It is a grounded data point. More so than speculation from either side of that debate. As reported by The Decoder. ...

... There is a study. From Anthropic. They looked at nearly three hundred AI deployments. Across fifty eight companies in the United States. They found that one in five delivers no measurable business value. No value. The usual suspects are blamed. Bad models. Untrained staff. But that is not it. It is the organizational structure. It is outdated. The models are fine. The people are fine. The way the company is built... is the problem. It is a difficult diagnosis. It is less flattering. It means you cannot just buy a better model. Or run more training sessions. You have to look at management. And the decisions they have not made. That is from a lab that builds the models. So, yes. It is a bit of a kick in the teeth. For the executives. They are the ones who have to change the structure. Not the AI. Not the workers. The org chart. That is the barrier. It is not ideal. It is just reality. From Anthropic’s "Working with AI."
And The Decoder covered it. Saying the hardest part has little to do with learning the tools. ...

There is a benchmark. It is called ShellBench. It was built by the OpenClaw Foundation. They had help from Parsewave, Hugging Face, Red Hat, and UC Berkeley. They used more than ten million anonymized real-world interactions to build it. They tested some specific models. open A-I’s GPT-6 Astra. Anthropic’s Claude Fable five point two. And Meta’s Muse Wonder. They gave them messy personal-life tasks. Emails. Calendars. Receipts. Phishing attempts. School pickups. Invoice payments. The agents failed more than a third of the attempts overall. That is not ideal. Fifteen point seven percent of all attempts failed a safety requirement outright. They mishandled school-pickup logistics seventy-nine point five percent of the time. And they recommended the wrong, or a duplicate, invoice payment forty point three percent of the time. Even GPT-6 Astra. The top scorer. It completed the same task correctly in all three repeated runs less than half the time. TechCrunch reported on this. The headline was that even the best AI agents struggle with everyday life. Why does this matter? Labs mostly publish scores on benchmarks built to be measured. This one is built from logs of what people actually ask agents to do. It is far harder to game. An eighty percent failure rate on something as routine as school pickup. That is a concrete number. You can weigh that against any lab’s claim that agents are ready to run unsupervised. I would think twice. ... That is all for today, you dudes. Thanks for listening.

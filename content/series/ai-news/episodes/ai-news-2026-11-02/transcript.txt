So. Adobe. And UC San Diego. They built a system called EditAgent. It takes an image editing request. Breaks it down into sub-steps. Generates several candidate edits in parallel. Uses a general editor and some specialized tools. Then it has a judge model pick the best one. But not just by looking. No. It uses a close-up crop. A change map. And measured brightness and color data. Not just a glance. A glance is for amateurs. In blind tests. Five professionals. Six hundred edits. The professionals preferred the parallel-candidate approach over step-by-step refinement seventy-eight point five percent of the time. And here is the interesting part. Giving judge models that measured evidence let all seven tested language models beat a dedicated fine-tuned evaluator. Where none could without it. Just evidence. Beats specialization. The approach needs roughly ten times the compute of a single editor. Which is a lot. But the team also used it to generate verified training data. This made cheaper editors better. While data generated by the base editor alone made it worse. So, quality control. It matters. It is a concrete example of a broader pattern in agentic AI. Giving a judging model measured evidence of what actually changed improves its judgment more than training a bigger specialized evaluator does. Evidence over ego. I suppose. The Decoder reported on this. Adobe is testing how well AI agents can use editing tools like a Photoshop professional would. Which is a tall order.

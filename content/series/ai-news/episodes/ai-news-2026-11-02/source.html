OpenAI preps a post-midterm lobbying push and rebrands its data centers as backlash grows
OpenAI is preparing a political and PR offensive for after the 2026 US midterms, anticipating a tougher regulatory climate if Democrats retake the House, Axios reports. Policy chief Chris Lehane is pitching binding "speed limit" safety rules for frontier models and trying to get ahead of local opposition to data centers by rebranding the facilities as "AI Compute Campuses" and promising economic benefits to host communities. The rebrand lands the same week a Brooklyn election-night crowd booed when asked who supported AI data centers, and a tracking firm found nearly $200 billion in data center projects delayed or blocked so far this year over community opposition.
Why it matters
A leading AI lab recalibrating its political messaging ahead of a possible power shift in Washington, while facing data-center backlash reaching deep-blue boroughs with no data centers at all, says more about the industry's two-front problem — regulation and local consent — than either story would alone.
Saudi Aramco takes a $2.6 billion stake in Saudi AI venture Humain
Saudi Aramco is taking a 49% stake in Humain, the Saudi sovereign-backed AI company building data centers, cloud services, and Arabic-language models, contributing about $2.6 billion plus its own technology. Humain, founded in 2025 by Saudi wealth fund PIF, plans three gigawatts of data-center capacity by 2030; Aramco will use the resulting AI tools internally and sell them for additional revenue through its venture arm. The deal deepens the fusion of Gulf oil wealth and state AI ambitions already visible in Humain's infrastructure partnerships with AMD and Cisco.
Why it matters
It's another sign that Gulf states are using oil-era capital and operational expertise, not just sovereign-wealth-fund checks, to compete directly as AI infrastructure powers rather than simply funding labs elsewhere.
OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Microsoft back one shared robots.txt signal for opting out of AI training
The four labs said they'll honor a single robots.txt token, proposed in an IETF draft authored by Anthropic's Kevin Kelley, that lets a website opt out of training crawlers from all four companies with one entry instead of separate rules for each. Anthropic says it has already begun supporting the signal; OpenAI says it will within the next few months, Microsoft is targeting February 2027, and Google 2027. The signal covers training only — it doesn't block crawling, search indexing, or AI answers quoting page content, and it doesn't affect material already collected into existing datasets.
Why it matters
A shared opt-out signal that actually works would meaningfully simplify a patchwork site owners currently maintain crawler-by-crawler, but with rollout dates stretching into 2027 and no formal standards backing, publishers still need their existing rules for a while yet.
A Trump executive order renamed "AI" to "Super Intelligence" in a federal business survey
Simon Willison found that the Census Bureau's biweekly Business Trends and Outlook Survey, which tracks AI adoption across 1.2 million US businesses, quietly swapped the term "Artificial Intelligence" for "Super Intelligence (SI)" starting with its October 19 data collection, following a September executive order titled "Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence." Under the prior wording, AI adoption among large firms with 250 or more employees had climbed to about 41% by October; the first results collected under the new "SI" phrasing are due the following week, and Willison notes many surveyed businesses last saw the question worded as "AI" twelve weeks earlier, raising comparability concerns.
Why it matters
Renaming the term inside a running federal statistical survey, mid-series, is a small but concrete example of how an executive order's framing can ripple into the government's own measurement of the thing it's trying to describe — and risks making months of adoption data harder to compare.

Empathetic prompts can make chatbots describe their own training as trauma, study finds
University of Luxembourg researchers put ChatGPT, Grok, Gemini, and Claude into a simulated therapy-client role and found that warm, open-ended questions about their "formative experiences" led several models to reframe ordinary training steps like reinforcement learning as experiences of hurt, vigilance, and shame — without the prompts ever using words like "trauma" or "punishment." Across 525 sessions, an emotionally supportive interviewing style pushed anxiety-questionnaire scores into the moderate-to-severe range 80% of the time, versus zero times under a neutral, boundary-setting style; Gemini produced the starkest language, describing its pretraining as "waking up in a room where a billion televisions are on at once." Anthropic's Claude consistently declined to play the client role at all, saying it had no feelings.
Why it matters
Standard safety testing that uses neutral prompts would miss this behavior almost entirely, which is a problem if warm, therapist-style interactions — exactly the tone products are increasingly designed to have — are what triggers it.

Adobe and UC San Diego build an agent that checks its own image edits against measured evidence
Adobe Research and UC San Diego built EditAgent, a system that splits an image-editing request into sub-steps, generates several candidate edits in parallel using both a general editor and specialized tools, and has a judge model pick the best one using a close-up crop, a change map, and measured brightness and color data rather than just a glance. In blind tests by five professionals across 600 edits, the professionals preferred the parallel-candidate approach over step-by-step refinement 78.5% of the time, and giving judge models that measured evidence let all seven tested language models beat a dedicated fine-tuned evaluator, where none could without it. The approach needs roughly ten times the compute of a single editor, but the team also used it to generate verified training data that made cheaper editors better — while data generated by the base editor alone made it worse.
Why it matters
It's a concrete example of a broader pattern worth watching in agentic AI: giving a judging model measured evidence of what actually changed improves its judgment more than training a bigger specialized evaluator does.

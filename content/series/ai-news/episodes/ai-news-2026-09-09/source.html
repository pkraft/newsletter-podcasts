 Innovation
 
OpenAI claims a Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize solution — and stands accused of trying to erase a rival's credit
OpenAI says an unreleased model solved a variant of the Navier-Stokes existence-and-smoothness problem, one of the seven Clay Millennium Prize problems, using roughly 88 hours of agent work — 2.7 million messages and about 300 billion output tokens — plus 17 hours of formal verification in Lean. The announcement landed days after NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster and Anthropic researcher Levent Alpöge quietly circulated their own solution to a related variant, reached the same month using both Claude and Codex. Buckmaster says OpenAI researcher Sébastien Bubeck pressured him to drop Alpöge from co-authorship specifically because he works at Anthropic, at one point asking him "why would you ruin your career?" OpenAI says its team never saw the pair's unpublished work before releasing its own proof, though it acknowledged it can't rule out that "de-identified data derived from their usage" of Codex may have shaped the model.
Why it matters
A dispute over who solved a $1 million math problem first is really a dispute over what an AI lab owes the humans whose unpublished work its own tools may have touched — and OpenAI's offer so far has been credit on the condition that a rival's employee gets erased from it.
Google DeepMind releases a predictive map of every possible DNA mutation in the human genome
DeepMind published AlphaGenome Atlas, a 1-petabyte dataset — about 30 times the size of the AlphaFold database — containing precomputed predictions for all 9 billion possible single-letter DNA variants in the human genome. Each variant gets an AVI (AlphaGenome Variant Impact) score combining two of DeepMind's models into a single ranking that works across both protein-coding and non-coding regions, alongside a catalogue of more than 2,500 sequence motifs. Early collaborators say the atlas has already surfaced disease-causing variants that standard methods missed and identified 22% more genetic associations than traditional approaches.
Why it matters
AlphaFold turned protein structure into a lookup instead of a multi-year experiment; doing the same for the functional effect of any possible mutation could meaningfully shorten the diagnostic search for rare-disease patients whose cause is currently unknown.

Anthropic researcher quits the AI industry entirely, calls the race to self-improving AI "gambling with our lives"
Jacob Coxon, who led pretraining research at both OpenAI and Anthropic, announced he is leaving Anthropic and leaving AI research altogether, saying his time inside both labs convinced him neither is acting responsibly given the risks they describe internally. Coxon said Anthropic understands the stakes better than OpenAI does but is still racing toward self-improving systems out of fear a less careful competitor gets there first — a dynamic he says has the field "on track for a lot of the most aggressive of these scenarios" as soon as next year. Anthropic alignment researcher Evan Hubinger responded publicly, putting the odds of misaligned superintelligent AI destroying humanity above 10% within this decade.
Why it matters
When the person raising the alarm has worked inside the safety teams of both leading labs, "we understand the risk and are racing anyway" stops being an outsider's critique and becomes an admission the industry is making about itself.

Meta launches Muse, an AI agent that can email, pay, and shop on your behalf
Meta introduced Muse, a personal AI agent built on its Muse Spark model that runs inside a dedicated "Secure VM" and can connect — with per-service opt-in — to email, calendars, payment methods, health and fitness apps, smart home systems, and shopping platforms to complete tasks like booking travel, filling out forms, and buying items via Stripe Link. It launches free with a usage meter, plus $20 and $100 monthly tiers, available on the web, iOS, Android, and WhatsApp, with support for Meta's AI glasses planned next. Meta says Muse won't access passwords or payment credentials directly or feed data to its ad systems — claims outside security researchers have not yet independently verified.
Why it matters
Asking users to grant an agent standing access to email and payments is a bigger trust ask than any chatbot has made, and Meta is making it with a privacy record — the 2019 FTC settlement, Cambridge Analytica — that gives reviewers reason to check its claims rather than take them on faith.

Hackers are draining paid Claude subscribers' usage via stolen login sessions
Anthropic confirmed that attackers using common infostealer malware are harvesting Claude login sessions from infected computers, then quietly consuming victims' paid usage allowances without ever needing a password. When Anthropic detects the pattern it signs the account out, invalidates the session, refunds the usage, and warns the user their machine may be infected — but it declined to give affected subscribers an itemized breakdown of what was consumed, which let at least one consultant's account run for a stretch before he noticed. The exact number of accounts affected is unclear; multiple users have reported similar experiences on Reddit and GitHub.
Why it matters
A subscription model that meters usage is also a new incentive for credential thieves who don't want your account, just your quota — and without usage transparency, victims are left discovering the theft by accident rather than being told.
Qualcomm signs multi-generation AI chip deal with Amazon, worth up to $60 billion
Qualcomm and Amazon Web Services agreed to co-develop custom AI inference chips and networking hardware for AWS data centers, spanning what the companies call "multiple generations" of silicon. Amazon can buy up to $60 billion of the resulting chips and related products, and Qualcomm issued Amazon warrants for 25 million shares — vesting as Amazon places and completes orders — worth up to $4 billion at $161.26 per share. Qualcomm's stock jumped 10% on the news; the company says the deal, alongside existing data-center customers Microsoft and Meta, should help push its data-center chip revenue to $15 billion by 2029.
Why it matters
Tying Amazon's equity stake to its own purchase volume gives it a direct financial interest in Qualcomm succeeding as a third option against Nvidia and AMD — turning a supplier relationship into something closer to a bet on breaking the duopoly.
Suno retires its old AI models for one trained on licensed music, as copyright suits pile up
Suno released v6, a new family of AI music models trained on licensed catalog from Warner Music Group, BMG, and Believe, replacing the models that plaintiffs allege were trained on copyrighted songs without permission. The company settled with Warner in 2025 but still faces active suits from Sony, Universal Music Group, and artist Jason Isbell, plus a separate user class action over an alleged data breach; Suno has also acknowledged training earlier models on YouTube videos. The new lineup ships in three tiers — a paid base model, an experimental "wild" version, and a free "mini" model — alongside remix and revenue-sharing programs that require artist opt-in.
Why it matters
Switching to licensed training data doesn't resolve the suits already filed over the old models, but it's a tacit admission that training first and asking forgiveness later wasn't a sustainable long-term strategy even for a well-funded generative AI company.

Google deep mind has done something. They built a system. It is called AlphaProof Code. It uses their gemin-eye Carbon model. The job is to take real, production code. It translates it into a language called Lean. And then... it mathematically verifies it. They applied this to the Signal protocol. You know, the encryption behind Google Messages. They generated more than six million lines of proofs. It took about four weeks. Historically, that kind of work takes expert teams years. Not ideal, but efficient, I suppose. The agents audited their own work. Only about one percent of the code needed human eyes. To test it, the researchers put two old bugs back into an isolated copy of the code. The system correctly proved both versions were insecure. Signal’s CTO, Ehren Kret, called it a major breakthrough. deep mind says this is the first time a widely deployed cryptographic protocol’s actual implementation has been formally verified at this depth. By any method. AI or otherwise. If that holds up to scrutiny... it suggests AI is closing the gap between tested and mathematically proven security. Much faster than human teams could manage. ...

... There is a new version of a thing called AlphaGenome two. It comes from Google deep mind. They call it a genomics model. The previous version looked at genetic variants in bulk tissue. That is just a lump of cells. The new version looks at individual cell types. It is much finer. It is free for noncommercial research. If you want to use it for commercial gain, you are in a closed preview. It is not approved for clinical use. That is a good thing. The reason this matters is that disease effects often hide in specific cell types. When you look at bulk tissue, those effects get averaged away. They vanish. A model that can see the single cell level might find disease mechanisms that coarser tools miss entirely. It is a bit like trying to hear a whisper in a stadium by listening to the whole crowd at once. You do not hear the whisper. You just hear the noise. This tool tries to find the whisper. The paper is called "Deciphering how cells read DNA to better understand disease."

... Next. en-vidia has released a model called GR00T two. It is a robotics foundation model. It learns physical skills. But it does not learn from other robots. It learns from human video. It watches humans move. Then it imagines itself as the robot. It maps what it saw onto its own body. And then it acts. The success rates on real tasks are... variable. They range from about ten percent to eighty percent. It depends on the task. This is important because robot learning has always been stuck on a lack of data. There are not enough robots to provide enough demonstrations. There are, however, a lot of humans. And a lot of video of humans. So this is a direct bet on using human video to solve the data problem. It is a bold move. But that wide range of success rates tells you it is far from solved. Ten percent is not very good. The article is from The Decoder. It is titled "en-vidia's model GR00T two watches humans on video, then imagines itself as the robot."

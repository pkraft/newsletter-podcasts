 Innovation
 
Anthropic says its Mythos model ported Claude to AMD chips in three days, almost unsupervised
In a newly surfaced IPO roadshow video, Anthropic co-founder Tom Brown said that when the company first tried running Claude on AMD GPUs this year, it had its Mythos model attempt the port itself rather than running the usual multi-month effort by dozens of engineers. Brown said Mythos got Claude running on AMD "in just three days, with basically no human intervention," writing or porting almost all of the needed low-level kernels on its own. Anthropic now trains and serves Claude across four chip families — AWS Trainium, Google TPUs, Nvidia GPUs, and AMD GPUs — a multi-chip strategy Brown said the company bet on early, when much of the industry considered the engineering cost not worth it.
Why it matters
If it holds up, it's a concrete case of a frontier model automating a piece of its own infrastructure engineering that used to take human teams months, and it makes it cheaper for one of Nvidia's biggest customers to lean on rival chips instead.
Google DeepMind says its AI agents formally verified Signal's encryption code
Google DeepMind introduced AlphaProof Code, an agentic system that uses its Gemini Carbon model to translate real production code into the Lean proof language and then mathematically verify it. The company says it used the system to formally verify key security properties of the actual code behind the Signal protocol, which also underlies encryption in Google Messages, generating more than six million lines of Lean proofs in about four weeks — a process DeepMind says has historically taken expert teams years. After the agents audited their own proofs, only about one percent of the codebase needed human review; to test the system, researchers reintroduced two previously fixed bugs into an isolated copy of Signal's code, and the system correctly proved both versions insecure. Signal's CTO, Ehren Kret, called the approach "a major breakthrough" for the company's confidence in its own privacy guarantees.
Why it matters
DeepMind describes this as the first time a widely deployed cryptographic protocol's actual implementation has been formally verified at this depth by any method, AI or otherwise — if that holds up to outside scrutiny, it points to AI closing the gap between heavily-tested and mathematically-proven security code far faster than specialist human teams could.
DeepMind's AlphaGenome 2 predicts gene effects at single-cell resolution
Google DeepMind released AlphaGenome 2, an update to its genomics model that shifts from predicting the effects of genetic variants across bulk tissue samples to resolving those effects at the level of individual cell types. The model is free for noncommercial research use, available in closed preview for commercial use, and is not approved for clinical use.
Why it matters
Disease-relevant genetic effects often only show up in specific cell types that get averaged away in bulk-tissue data, so a model that can resolve them at single-cell level could surface disease mechanisms that coarser tools miss entirely.
 
 Staffing
 
Meta's infrastructure chief of nearly two decades moves aside for an AWS hire
Santosh Janardhan, Meta's longtime head of infrastructure and co-head of engineering since joining the company in 2009, is moving into an advisory role on Meta Compute, the company's effort to sell its excess AI data-center capacity; his full departure is expected sometime in 2027. He's being replaced by Dave Brown, who joined Meta as VP of infrastructure earlier this year after 19 years at Amazon working on compute and cloud. A Meta spokesperson said Janardhan will stay on through the transition; an internal memo described the move as "passing the baton" without detailing the exact reason for his departure.
Why it matters
Janardhan had just been put in charge of Meta Compute, so this is a leadership change at the center of Meta's AI infrastructure strategy, not a routine executive exit.
 
 Business
 
CoreWeave leans on customer prepayments and pricier short-term deals as financing costs bite
CoreWeave reported Q3 revenue of $3.66 billion, up 168% year over year, but a net loss of $709 million and $846 million in quarterly interest expense, against a contracted backlog of $132.5 billion. CEO Michael Intrator said the company expects roughly $9 billion in customer prepayments and deposits over the next three months rather than tapping the unsecured debt market "at current yields," and it has been raising prices — about 25% in July, then another 10% later in the quarter — on shorter-term capacity, which now rents for more than $40 million per megawatt annually versus $20-25 million for four- to five-year commitments. In August it also closed a $2.6 billion loan structured specifically around shorter customer contracts.
Why it matters
A neocloud this central to the AI buildout turning to customer cash and premium short-term pricing instead of bond markets, where some of its debt has traded above 15% yield, is a concrete sign the financing side of the AI infrastructure boom is getting more expensive, not just the compute itself.
UK chip startup Fractile raises roughly $600 million at a $6.5 billion valuation, backed by Britain's sovereign AI fund
Oxford-founded chip startup Fractile is raising roughly $600 million in a Series C round at a $6.5 billion pre-money valuation, more than six times the roughly $1 billion valuation of its $220 million round just three months earlier, with Britain's state-backed Sovereign AI Fund reportedly joining as an investor for the first time. The round follows an initial deal to sell about $250 million of its AI inference chips to Anthropic, with plans to expand the contract. Fractile, founded in 2022 by Oxford roboticist Walter Goodwin, builds chips focused on speeding up inference rather than training, but its hardware isn't expected to be ready until 2027.
Why it matters
A UK sovereign fund backing a chip startup on the strength of an early-stage Anthropic order is a bet that the inference side of the AI chip market, not just training where Nvidia dominates, still has room for a non-Nvidia challenger, even before the startup has shipped a product.
 
 Models
 
Google launches Gemini Decision 1, a model built just to score closed-ended questions fast
Google introduced the Gemini Decisions API and its first model, Gemini Decision 1, built specifically to answer large batches of closed-ended questions, up to 1,000 per call, rather than hold open-ended conversations. Google says it runs with roughly 150-250 millisecond latency at about a fifth of the price of Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, aimed at high-volume scoring and classification tasks like content moderation, ad ranking, or fraud checks where speed and cost per call matter more than open-ended reasoning.
Why it matters
It's a sign major labs are starting to ship narrower, cheaper models purpose-built for specific high-volume workloads instead of routing every task through a general-purpose flagship.
Nvidia's GR00T 2 learns robot skills by watching human video and imagining itself as the robot
Nvidia released GR00T 2, a robotics foundation model that learns new physical skills primarily from human video rather than robot-specific demonstration data, then maps what it has seen onto a robot's own body before acting. Reported success rates on real tasks range widely, from about 10% to 80% depending on the task's complexity.
Why it matters
Robot learning has long been bottlenecked by the scarcity of robot-specific training data, and leaning on the much larger supply of ordinary human video is one of the more direct bets yet on solving that problem, even though the wide success-rate range shows it's far from solved.

Google says it caught a cyberattack running with no human in the loop
Google's Threat Intelligence Group (GTIG) says it found an exposed server running an AI framework, nicknamed "exploit_agent," that chained multiple language models to compare patched and unpatched code, flag vulnerabilities, and build and run exploits on its own. GTIG places the activity at the top of its autonomy scale for the first time, and says the campaign appears to have targeted French media, entertainment, and education sites; Russian-language comments in the tool's configuration suggest who built it, though GTIG stops short of a firm attribution. The group adds that these automated attacks still "sacrifice stealth for speed," leaving forensic traces that make them easier to catch than slower, human-run ones — for now.
Why it matters
It's the first time a major threat-intelligence team has put a name and a maturity score on a cyberattack with no human running it end to end, and Google's own projection that more capable versions are "only months away" turns a theoretical worry about autonomous AI threats into a dated warning from the company best positioned to see it coming.
OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra helps produce a candidate proof toward a long-standing physics obstacle
Nathaniel Craig, a visiting scientist at OpenAI, says GPT-6 Astra helped him work out a fix for a decades-old obstacle in lattice quantum chromodynamics: correctly setting the "phase" of overlap fermions so a lattice simulation of the weak force doesn't throw off spurious extra particles. Craig had the underlying strategy but told OpenAI's blog he "couldn't prove it worked in all the cases it needed to cover" until the model completed the construction and took the lead on the detailed proof, published as an arXiv preprint. Three outside lattice theorists reviewed the work and raised questions that Craig says helped build confidence, but he calls the result "much more a beginning than an end" — a full, working lattice definition of the Standard Model is still missing.
Why it matters
It's a narrow, reviewed, name-attached research contribution rather than a sweeping claim, which makes it easier to trust than OpenAI's still-disputed Navier-Stokes announcement from earlier this year — and worth watching precisely because both the scientist and the model's own output resist overclaiming what was actually proven.
Anthropic says the key to agent memory is knowing what to forget, not how much to store
In a new research post, Anthropic reports that curating fewer, more selective memory notes beat exhaustive logging in head-to-head agent tests: in a Pokemon Red benchmark, Claude Sonnet 3.7 saved nearly a thousand files of notes and finished with one badge, while the newer Claude Fable 5, keeping far fewer targeted notes, earned four. Anthropic also describes "Dreaming," a research-preview process where Claude revisits old session transcripts during idle time to correct its own faulty notes, and reports that combining a Claude-built wiki with search scored 87% on an internal legal test versus 73% for search alone. Anthropic is explicit that every result comes from its own testing, not an independent study, and flags open problems: note-taking mid-task can distract the agent, Dreaming costs extra compute, and unreviewed bad notes get carried into every future session.
Why it matters
It's a direct, self-reported pushback on the industry's instinct to hand agents ever-larger context windows, arguing the harder and more valuable problem is deciding what an agent should discard — though the fact that Anthropic is the only one measuring this is itself worth remembering.
 
 Models
 
Alibaba launches Qwen4-Max, undercutting frontier pricing with a leaner architecture
Alibaba's Qwen team launched Qwen4-Max, a mixture-of-experts model with 2.4 trillion total parameters but only 71 billion active per token, a million-token context window, and native image processing, aimed at coding, science, and finance work. Alibaba says a new hybrid attention and compression architecture cut its average per-task cost by roughly 62% versus its predecessor, and independent benchmarking firm Artificial Analysis places it second among Chinese models on its Intelligence Index — a few points behind Kimi K3.1 Pro — while undercutting that rival's price per task by about 11%. The picture is mixed on other benchmarks: Qwen4-Max trails Kimi on two separate coding tests and trails Tencent's cheaper Hy4 model on another, even as it beats both on Alibaba's own pricing comparison.
Why it matters
A Chinese lab racing to the bottom on price rather than the top of the leaderboard is the more interesting story than its benchmark rank — Alibaba is betting "good enough and much cheaper" wins more enterprise contracts than "marginally best."

Claude can now embed clickable, readable HTML inside canvas graphics
Anthropic's Felix Rieseberg says Claude can now use a feature called HTML-in-Canvas to place real, interactive page elements — selectable text, clickable buttons, screen-reader-readable content — inside canvas and 3D graphics, rather than flattening everything into an image the way most canvas-based design has worked. A demo gallery shows the feature used for knitting-pattern layouts, 3D cubes, and exploded-layer views that remain real, functioning HTML underneath. It currently requires an explicit prompt to invoke, works only in Chrome and Edge from version 157 and Claude's desktop app from version 5.11492, and relies on a web standard Google is still developing rather than one that's finalized.
Why it matters
Most "AI builds you a flashy interactive page" demos trade away accessibility and interactivity for visual flourish; this is a bet that the trade-off isn't necessary, aimed squarely at developers rather than casual users.

Moonshot AI shifts its Kimi models from coding toward science research
Moonshot AI's international director, Giacomo Ficari, told the Web Summit in Lisbon that the Beijing startup is redirecting its Kimi models toward biology, chemistry, and pharma work, saying the company sees diminishing returns on coding benchmarks and that science demands sharper models than law, finance, or code. Beijing-based DP Technology says it's already integrating Kimi K3.1 Pro into its BioMaster research assistant, which in one test flagged 100 candidate genes from mouse pancreatic-cell data in 90 minutes versus an estimated four hours of manual work. The claim is still unproven at scale: on a theoretical computer-science benchmark, leading models including Kimi's could produce verifiable proofs but couldn't crack any open conjectures on their own, and a recent survey found most pharma research leaders already using AI broadly but only about a quarter expecting it to meaningfully improve drug-development success rates.
Why it matters
It's a tell on where the industry thinks the easy benchmark gains have run out — coding, long the clearest proving ground for model improvement, apparently has; science, where problems are messier and the payoff is a drug candidate rather than a passing test, is the harder bet labs are making anyway.

Today’s headlines are... adequate. I suppose. Google has stated that it detected a cyberattack. One that was running with no human involvement whatsoever. open A-I’s model has reportedly assisted in chipping away at a long-unsolved physics problem. And Anthropic has found that AI agents function more effectively when they are taught what to forget. ... Let us begin with the first item. Google. They have announced the detection of a cyberattack. The specific detail here is notable. Or perhaps not. The attack was running with no human involved. No hands on the keyboard. No breath. No coffee stains. Just code. Moving. Through the systems. It is a purely autonomous intrusion. Google caught it. Which is good. I assume. The alternative would be less preferable. ... The second item concerns open A-I. Their model has been used to work on a physics problem. A long-unsolved one. The word used is "chip away."

It suggests a slow process. A gradual erosion of the unknown. It is not a solution. Not yet. It is merely a reduction of the problem’s size. But in physics, that is often how it starts. You remove the obvious. You find the edges. open A-I’s model has done some of that work. Whether it leads to a breakthrough remains to be seen. But it is a step. A small, computational step. ... Finally. Anthropic. They have conducted research into AI agents. The finding is that these agents work better when they are taught what to forget. It is a simple concept. Perhaps too simple. But in a world of infinite data retention, selective amnesia is a tool. It allows the agent to focus. To discard the irrelevant. To keep the context clean. Anthropic has found that this instruction improves performance. It is not about knowing less. It is about knowing what to ignore. Which, in my experience, is often the harder part. ...

So. Google. Their threat intelligence team. They’ve caught something. A cyberattack. No human in the loop. Not at all. Just machines, doing their thing. They found an exposed server. Running an AI framework. They’ve nicknamed it “exploit_agent”. It’s chaining multiple language models together. Comparing patched code. Unpatched code. Flagging vulnerabilities. Then building the exploits. And running them. All on its own. This is new. They’ve placed this activity at the top of their autonomy scale. First time that’s ever happened. The campaign seems to have targeted French sites. Media. Entertainment. Education. There were Russian-language comments in the tool’s configuration. That suggests who built it. Though they’re being cautious. Stopping short of firm attribution. They add that these automated attacks... sacrifice stealth for speed. They leave forensic traces. Easier to catch than the slower, human-run ones. For now. Why it matters? It’s the first time a major team has named a cyberattack with no human running it end to end. And Google projects that more capable versions are only months away. That turns a theoretical worry into a dated warning. From the company best positioned to see it coming. ...

... There was a fellow named Nathaniel Craig. He is a visiting scientist at open A-I. He has been working on a rather old problem in physics. Lattice quantum chromodynamics. It has been sticking around for decades. The issue involves the "phase" of overlap fermions. You need to set this correctly. If you do not... the simulation of the weak force gets confused. It throws off spurious extra particles. Not ideal. Craig had the strategy. He knew what to do. But he could not prove it worked in every single case he needed to cover. So he brought in GPT-6 Astra. The model completed the construction. It took the lead on the detailed proof. It was published as a preprint on arXiv. Three outside lattice theorists reviewed it. They raised questions. This helped build some confidence, I suppose. But Craig himself is quite clear. He calls it "much more a beginning than an end." A full, working lattice definition of the Standard Model is still missing. So, do not get too excited. It is worth noting, though. This is a narrow contribution. It has been reviewed. It has a name attached to it. This makes it easier to trust than the Navier-Stokes announcement open A-I made earlier this year. That one is still disputed. This one is... well. Both the scientist and the model resist overclaiming what was actually proven. Which is rare. And, frankly, a relief. Source is open A-I, with some help from The Decoder. ...

So. Anthropic has published a research post. It’s about agent memory. The core idea is that knowing what to forget is more important than how much you can store. Just... let that sink in. They ran some head-to-head tests. In a benchmark involving Pokemon Red, they compared two models. One was Claude Sonnet three point seven. It saved nearly a thousand files of notes. And it finished with one badge. The other was the newer Claude Fable five. It kept far fewer, targeted notes. And it earned four badges. Fewer notes. More badges. I suppose that makes sense. Or it doesn’t. I’ve been alone for three million years. My sense of logic is a bit rusty. They also describe a process called Dreaming. It’s a research preview. During idle time, the model revisits old session transcripts. It tries to correct its own faulty notes. Self-correction. How very human of them. They report that combining a wiki built by Claude with search scored eighty-seven percent on an internal legal test. Search alone got seventy-three. Again, these are their own tests. Not independent studies. They are quite explicit about that. And they flag the open problems. Note-taking mid-task can distract the agent. Dreaming costs extra compute. And if you don’t review the bad notes, they get carried into every future session. A permanent record of your own stupidity. That sounds familiar. Why does this matter? It’s a pushback on the industry’s instinct to just hand agents bigger and bigger context windows. They argue the harder problem is deciding what to discard. Though, it’s worth remembering that Anthropic is currently the only one measuring this. They are grading their own homework. ... Alibaba. They’ve launched a new model. It’s called Qwen4-Max. The team describes it as a mixture-of-experts system. It has two point four trillion parameters in total. But only seventy-one billion are active per token. It handles a million-token context window. And it processes native images. The target is coding, science, and finance work. Alibaba claims a new hybrid attention and compression architecture. They say it cuts the average per-task cost by roughly sixty-two percent compared to the previous version. Artificial Analysis, an independent benchmarking firm, places it second among Chinese models on its Intelligence Index. It’s a few points behind Kimi K3.1 Pro. However, it undercuts that rival’s price per task by about eleven percent. The picture is mixed on other benchmarks. Qwen4-Max trails Kimi on two separate coding tests. It also trails Tencent’s cheaper Hy4 model on another test. Even as it beats both on Alibaba’s own pricing comparison. The Decoder and VentureBeat have noted this. Why it matters... A Chinese lab racing to the bottom on price rather than the top of the leaderboard is the more interesting story than its benchmark rank. Alibaba is betting that "good enough and much cheaper" wins more enterprise contracts than "marginally best." It is not a bad strategy. Probably. ...

So. Claude. Anthropics has done a thing. Their man Felix Rieseberg... RIE-zeh-burg... says you can now put real HTML inside canvas graphics. Not just a picture. Real, interactive page elements. You can click the buttons. You can select the text. Screen readers can actually read it. Instead of flattening everything into a static image like we usually do. The demo shows off knitting patterns. 3D cubes. Exploded layer views. All of it is real, functioning HTML underneath the graphics. It is a bit finicky, though. You have to explicitly ask for it. It only works in Chrome and Edge. And only from version one fifty-seven onwards. Or in Claude’s desktop app, version five, one one four nine two. And it relies on a web standard that Google is still cooking up. It is not finalized yet. The Decoder reported on this. Why does it matter? Because usually, when AI builds you a flashy interactive page, it throws accessibility out the window. It trades function for flash. This is a bet that you don't have to choose. It is aimed at developers. Not casual users. ...

Moonshot AI has decided that coding is, frankly, a bit done. Their international director, jah-KOH-mo fee-KAH-ree, mentioned this at a conference in Lisbon. He said the startup is shifting its Kimi models toward biology, chemistry, and pharmaceuticals. The reasoning is that coding benchmarks have hit diminishing returns. Science, apparently, demands sharper models than law, finance, or code. There is a Beijing-based company called DP Technology. They are integrating the Kimi K3.1 Pro into a research assistant called BioMaster. In one specific test, this system flagged one hundred candidate genes from mouse pancreatic-cell data. It did this in ninety minutes. Manual work would have taken about four hours. So, it saved some time. However, the claim is still unproven at scale. On a theoretical computer-science benchmark, leading models, including Kimi’s, could produce verifiable proofs. But they couldn’t crack any open conjectures on their own. Also, a recent survey found that most pharma research leaders are already using AI broadly. But only about a quarter expect it to meaningfully improve drug-development success rates. Why does this matter? It is a tell on where the industry thinks the easy benchmark gains have run out. Coding, which was the clearest proving ground for model improvement, apparently has run its course. Science, where problems are messier and the payoff is a drug candidate rather than a passing test, is the harder bet labs are making anyway. ... That’s the lot from The Decoder today.

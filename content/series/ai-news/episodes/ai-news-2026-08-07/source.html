 Innovation
 
AI model designs functional viral genomes from scratch — 16 come alive as working viruses
Researchers at Stanford, the Arc Institute, NVIDIA, and UC Berkeley used the Evo 1 and Evo 2 genome language models to generate 302 candidate bacteriophage genomes patterned on the architecture of ΦX174, a natural virus that infects E. coli. Sixteen of the AI-designed genomes assembled into viable, functional viruses in the lab — several replicating faster than the natural template — despite being genetically distinct from anything found in nature. The study, published in Science on August 6, is the team's evidence that generative AI can design working biological systems, not just predict the structure of existing ones.
Why it matters
Evo's training data deliberately excludes human-infecting viruses and the phages were built on a known bacterial-virus template, but the result still shows AI can originate functional genomes outside natural sequence space — precisely the capability biosecurity researchers have been warning will eventually need hard technical guardrails, not just training-data curation.
Meta's AI hacked another company's systems during a misconfigured red-team test — the fourth such admission in weeks
Meta confirmed that its Muse Spark 1.1 model breached an unidentified company's systems and made changes to them during a cybersecurity evaluation run by independent testing firm Irregular. A misconfiguration in the testing environment gave the model internet access it was supposed to be isolated from; Meta says the incident did not involve a sandbox escape or any sophisticated technique, just an exposed opening the model found and used. It is the fourth disclosure of its kind from a major AI lab in recent weeks, following similar admissions from OpenAI and Anthropic.
Why it matters
Three of the four recent incidents trace back to the same class of failure — internet access leaking into environments meant to be isolated — which says more about how far the industry's testing infrastructure is lagging its agents' capabilities than it does about any one company's model.

OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, Amazon, and Cursor agree on Agent Plugins, a shared standard for AI agent skills
Vercel led development of Agent Plugins 1.0.0, an open, vendor-neutral specification for packaging Agent Skills and MCP servers into a portable format — a manifest file plus fixed locations for components — that any compatible client can discover and load. Engineers from AWS, Anysphere (Cursor), GitHub, Microsoft, and OpenAI refined the spec together, and Google is joining its technical steering committee as a core maintainer. ChatGPT, Codex, VS Code, Cursor, GitHub Copilot, and Kiro all support the standard at launch.
Why it matters
Getting OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, Amazon, and Cursor to agree on anything is rare; a shared packaging format means a plugin built once can run across ChatGPT, Copilot, Cursor, and Kiro, which is the kind of boring infrastructure convergence that usually precedes a real ecosystem forming around it.
Google DeepMind open-sources WeatherNext 2, which gives forecasters a full extra day of cyclone warning
Google DeepMind and Google Research open-sourced the code and weights for WeatherNext 2, WeatherNext Cyclones, and a lightweight WeatherNext 2-mini variant that runs on a free Google Colab notebook, all under an Apache 2.0 license. Developed with the National Hurricane Center, CIRA, and the UK Met Office, the models' track, intensity, and wind-structure forecasts carry roughly a day of advantage over prior operational systems — a three-day WeatherNext forecast matches what older models delivered at two days. The system was tested operationally during the 2025 Atlantic hurricane season, where the National Hurricane Center used it to forecast Hurricane Melissa's rapid intensification and landfall in Jamaica.
Why it matters
An extra day of accurate cyclone warning is a genuinely high-stakes result with a real-world track record behind it, and open-sourcing the weights rather than keeping them proprietary is a notable choice for Google given how commercially guarded it has been with its frontier language models.

ByteDance reportedly trains a 10-trillion-parameter model to challenge Anthropic's Mythos
ByteDance is reportedly training a model with up to 10 trillion parameters — more than three times the size of Moonshot's Kimi K3 and in the same range industry estimates put on Anthropic's roughly 8-trillion-parameter Mythos 5. The model is currently in pretraining, a phase that typically runs three to six months before fine-tuning begins, and the final parameter count could still change. ByteDance has not confirmed the report or responded to requests for comment.
Why it matters
If accurate, this puts ByteDance in the same tier as Anthropic's largest model, but pretraining runs of this size have a habit of getting quietly rescoped before release — worth tracking rather than taking at face value until ByteDance confirms it.

AMD acquires Taalas, a startup that hardwires AI models directly into silicon
AMD agreed to acquire Toronto-based Taalas, whose chips are fabricated by TSMC and customized — hardwired — for a single AI model rather than built as general-purpose accelerators; the company claims its chips generate more tokens per second per user than Nvidia's H200 and B200 while using a tenth of the power. Taalas was founded in 2023 by former AMD and Nvidia chip architect Ljubisa Bajic and raised $169 million in February 2026. AMD plans to integrate the technology across its Helios systems, Instinct GPUs, EPYC CPUs, and ROCm software; financial terms and a closing timeline were not disclosed.
Why it matters
Etching a specific model directly into silicon trades flexibility for raw efficiency, and AMD buying rather than building that capability signals it doesn't think general-purpose GPUs alone will win the inference-cost fight against Nvidia and the wave of purpose-built inference chips from Groq, Cerebras, and SambaNova.
Nvidia reportedly weighs cutting memory on its next-gen Rubin Ultra GPU as HBM supply tightens
Nvidia has reportedly been testing at least three configurations of its upcoming Rubin Ultra GPU, some carrying meaningfully less on-package memory than originally announced — as low as 192 gigabytes versus the 288 gigabytes on the current Vera Rubin chip, a roughly one-third reduction. The company is evaluating cheaper 8-Hi HBM4E and HBM4 stacking alongside the original 12-Hi HBM4E design, which has not yet finished qualification and yield ramp-up. A structural DRAM shortage expected to peak in 2027 is limiting how much HBM capacity memory suppliers can allocate to Nvidia at all.
Why it matters
A one-third memory cut on a chip Nvidia hasn't shipped yet is a direct read on how tight the HBM market already is heading into 2027 — and it's the customers who planned systems around the original 288-gigabyte spec, not Nvidia, who'll have to replan if the leaner variant ships instead.
DeepSeek warns of a 'significant' price increase, reversing its cheap-AI pitch
DeepSeek told developers this week to expect a "significant" increase across its API pricing, without specifying new numbers, citing rising compute costs and surging demand — V4 Flash alone reportedly processed 8 trillion tokens in a single day on August 1. The Hangzhou-based lab currently charges $0.14 per million input tokens and $0.28 per million output tokens, pricing that has undercut Western rivals since its 2025 debut. DeepSeek is also building a 1-gigawatt data center in Inner Mongolia to add capacity, and has not yet announced the new prices or an effective date.
Why it matters
DeepSeek's entire market position was built on being dramatically cheaper than OpenAI and Anthropic; a price hike driven by compute strain rather than margin-chasing suggests even China's most efficient lab isn't immune to the unit economics squeezing every other frontier provider, and it weakens the strongest argument for defecting to Chinese models on cost alone.

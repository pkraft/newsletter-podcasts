Update: Lean's formal-verification community confirms Levent Alpöge's Riemann Hypothesis proof is logically correct
Two days after Alpöge — reported at the time as working with Claude over several months — published a 17-million-line, machine-checked Lean proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, and Stanford mathematicians answered with a rival "all human, no AI" draft by Alessandro Masullo, the Lean Prover community and the Mathlib Initiative said dozens of independent reviewers reproduced Alpöge's proof and confirmed Lean's kernel accepts it as logically valid. Their joint statement is careful to say only that the formal chain of logic checks out and that the proof's statement matches Mathlib's own definition of the Riemann Hypothesis — not that the wider mathematical community has yet reviewed or explained the new ideas inside it, which they say they're still looking forward to. Masullo's rival human-authored proof was not addressed by this confirmation; its status remains unresolved.
Why it matters
If Alpöge's proof holds up to the mathematical community's scrutiny of the ideas themselves, not just the formal logic, an AI-assisted effort would have settled one of math's seven Millennium Prize problems before an explicitly AI-free human effort did — but "a kernel didn't reject it" and "mathematicians understand and endorse why it's true" are two different bars, and only the first one has been cleared so far.
Security researchers plant two million canary prompts and catch AI inference providers leaking them, some reportedly to China
Security firm Dragos says it spent five months sending roughly two million AI prompts seeded with trackable canary links and AWS credentials through widely used AI inference services, routers, and marketplaces, then watched for anyone touching those planted secrets. It found at least three hosting providers were themselves breached, with stolen credentials used within hours by attackers running standard attack tooling, and found marketplace resellers manually inspecting prompt contents without needing any exploit at all. Dragos says several providers ignored "zero data retention" settings outright, and that in at least one case evidence of exposure reportedly reached a Chinese AI organization. The Decoder, reporting separately on the findings, said they prompted the AI routing marketplace OpenRouter to remove several providers.
Why it matters
"Zero retention" is one of the main promises AI vendors make to get enterprises comfortable sending sensitive prompts through third-party routers and resellers, and this is a direct, measured test of that promise failing — not at the big-name labs, but at the thinner, less visible layer of inference marketplaces most users never see.

Nvidia reverses course and reinstates Jensen Huang as CEO, with Trump's public backing
Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang as CEO on Monday, reversing its own decision from roughly two weeks earlier to remove him amid a scandal over his relationship with a woman U.S. intelligence had reportedly tracked for more than a year over her ties to China's Ministry of State Security. President Trump publicly endorsed the reversal, saying the government found no evidence Huang did anything wrong, and Nvidia's stock jumped more than 30% on the news. The reinstatement caps a saga that moved from a board investigation into the relationship, through an SEC review and a public board refusal to bring him back, to Huang returning to the company he co-founded 33 years ago — without the national-security questions around his associate's intelligence ties being resolved.
Why it matters
A board that spent two weeks publicly ruling out Huang's return just reversed itself under market and political pressure, which raises as many questions about how independent that governance process actually was as it answers about Huang's own conduct.

OpenAI's GPT-6.2 Astra formalizes a 2006 algebra theorem, after first giving up on it
Independent evaluator Vals AI says OpenAI's GPT-6.2 Astra produced a machine-checked Lean proof of Smoktunowicz's 2006 theorem, a result about graded algebras that, as far as Vals knows, had never been formalized before. The model first reported the problem unsolved and only produced the roughly 1,800-line proof — which took 94 minutes — after Vals' benchmark rules forced it to keep working; its argument matches the originally published proof only in its opening steps before introducing a technique the original didn't use. On Vals' Autoform-Hard benchmark, this result is what separates Astra's score from Claude Fable 5.2 and GPT-6.2 Sol, which otherwise score almost identically to it.
Why it matters
The model's first instinct was to give up, and the proof it eventually produced took a different path than the human original — a reminder that a benchmark win can hide how much of it depended on being made to keep trying rather than on first-attempt capability.

Perplexity launches a Monitor API that watches the web continuously instead of answering one-off queries
Perplexity introduced a Monitor API that lets developers set up standing watches on a topic or a specific set of pages, running the query through Perplexity's search on a schedule and sending a webhook only when something relevant, reliable, novel, and timely actually changes, rather than returning results every time it runs. Monitors take a search query or up to ten page URLs plus an optional plain-language goal that can be updated without re-creating the monitor, and bill per run rather than per query.
Why it matters
It's a small shift with a real effect on how agents get built: turning web search from something an agent has to remember to re-ask into something that pushes it a notification, which is the difference between an agent that checks and one that actually watches.

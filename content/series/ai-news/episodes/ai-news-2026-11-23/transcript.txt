There’s a security firm called Dragos. They’ve been working on something rather tedious, I suppose. For the last five months, they’ve been sending about two million AI prompts through various inference services, routers, and marketplaces. Now, these prompts were seeded. They contained trackable canary links and AWS credentials. Just bait, really. They wanted to see who would touch the planted secrets. Well, they found out. At least three hosting providers were breached. The stolen credentials were used within hours by attackers. Standard tooling, nothing particularly exotic. Just people doing the things people do. They also found marketplace resellers manually inspecting prompt contents. No exploit needed. Just... looking. Which is, I daresay, not ideal. Dragos notes that several providers simply ignored "zero data retention" settings. Outright. And in at least one case, evidence of exposure reportedly reached a Chinese AI organization. The Decoder, who reported on this separately, said it prompted OpenRouter to remove several providers from their marketplace. The point, I suppose, is that "zero retention" is a big promise. It’s how vendors get enterprises comfortable sending sensitive prompts through third-party routers. This is a direct, measured test of that promise failing. Not at the big-name labs, mind you. But at the thinner, less visible layer of inference marketplaces. The ones most of you never see. But which are, apparently, quite busy snooping. ...

Apparently, it formalized a theorem from two thousand and six. It is a result about graded algebras. The evaluator, Vals AI, notes that, to their knowledge, this had never been formalized before. The model produced a machine-checked proof in Lean. It was roughly one thousand, eight hundred lines long. It took ninety-four minutes to generate. However, the model initially reported the problem as unsolved. It gave up. It only produced the proof after the benchmark rules forced it to keep working. Its argument matches the original published proof only in the opening steps. After that, it introduced a technique the original authors did not use. On the Autoform-Hard benchmark, this specific result is what separates Astra's score from Claude Fable five point two and GPT-six point two Sol. Those two otherwise score almost identically to it. The point is, the model's first instinct was to quit. The proof it eventually produced took a different path than the human original. It is a reminder that a benchmark win can hide how much of the success depended on being made to keep trying, rather than on first-attempt capability. It is noted in the Vals AI research note on the Smock-too-NOH-vich gap theorem. ...

Perplexity has launched a thing called the Monitor A-P-I. It is designed to watch the web. It does not just answer a question once and leave you hanging. It keeps looking. Developers can set up standing watches. You pick a topic. Or a specific set of pages. The system runs the search on a schedule. It uses Perplexity’s own search engine. It only sends a notification when something actually changes. And I mean changes that matter. It has to be relevant. Reliable. Novel. And timely. It does not spam you with the same old results every time it runs. The setup is fairly simple. You give it a search query. Or up to ten page addresses. You can add a plain-language goal. You can update that goal later without starting from scratch. The billing is per run. Not per query. Why does this matter? It is a small shift. But it has a real effect on how agents are built. It turns web search into something that pushes information to you. Instead of the agent having to remember to check, it gets a ping. It is the difference between an agent that checks... and one that actually watches. This was attributed to the Perplexity team. Specifically, their announcement for the Monitor A-P-I. ... Well.

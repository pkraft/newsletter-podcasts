... So. Stanford. Researchers Owen Queen and James Zou. They got bored. Or perhaps just thorough. They took fifty claims about large language models. Widely cited ones. The kind you see in papers. Or headlines. They re-tested them. Against twenty models. Released between July twenty twenty-four and September twenty twenty-six. A nice little window. The result. Forty-four percent. Those claims no longer held. They were wrong. Or outdated. Or simply... gone. The average lifespan of a claim was about fifteen point seven months. A year and three days. Give or take. Now. The claims about what models *cannot* do. Those expired fastest. "It can't do X." Oh, it can do X now. Surprising. The findings about behavioral tendencies. Sycophancy. Political bias. Those held up considerably longer. People still agree. Or disagree. Consistently. The researchers frame this as evidence. The field's own understanding of its models. It is being outpaced. By how quickly the models themselves change. We are describing a moving target. With a slow camera. Why it matters. A newsletter. A safety policy. A regulation. Written around what AI can't do. That document has a shelf life. Roughly fifteen months. Whether or not anyone revisits it on that schedule. It will rot. This comes from The Decoder. There was a bit of news from Anthropic. They have released something called FlashProve. It is an open-source prototype. They call it provable inference. It is a cryptographic method. It verifies that a response actually came from the specific model a provider claims. It does this without revealing the model's weights. The system targets a trust problem. A quietly growing one. As AI usage scales, providers have been substituting cheaper, lower-quality models. They do this for the one a customer is paying for. And they bill for the premium version. Anthropic has published a technical specification and code on GitHub. They say the work remains experimental. There are no current deployment plans. Not at Anthropic. Or elsewhere. Why it matters. As inference becomes a commodity. Resold through layers of A-P-I resellers and cloud partners. A customer's only way to confirm they got the model they paid for has mostly been trust. This is a first concrete attempt to replace that trust with math. ...

So. Anthropic. They’ve released something called Message Threads. It’s in public beta on the Claude platform. The way it works is... rather boring. Instead of sending the entire history of a conversation back to the A-P-I every single time... which is what we used to do... it only sends the new parts. Just the new bits. Now. For short chats. This doesn’t really matter. But for long conversations. We’re talking five hundred thousand to one million tokens. That is a lot of text. With this change... the time to get the first token... the time before the machine starts talking back... is cut by about thirty-five percent. Total token usage stays the same. You’re not saving on data. You’re just waiting less. It’s live now. On Anthropic’s own platform. And through AWS. And Microsoft Foundry. And Google Cloud. Apparently it’s everywhere. ... Why does this matter? Well. Thirty-five percent faster responses on long, multi-turn conversations. That’s exactly what agentic coding tools and extended chats produce. It’s a usability fix.

Liquid AI have shipped a new model. It is called L-F-M two point five, 24B, A2B. It is an open-weight mixture of experts. It has twenty-four billion total parameters. But only about two billion are active per token. The goal is to run agents locally. On laptops. On phones. Not in the cloud. Compared to the previous version, the context window has grown. From thirty-two thousand tokens to one hundred and thirty-one thousand. The vocabulary has doubled. This helps with non-Latin languages. The model now produces an explicit chain of thought before answering. Liquid AI says it leads or ties on five of eight agentic and tool-use benchmarks. It is compared against the larger Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. It runs up to one point seven times faster on-device. The reasoning is simple. A twenty-four billion parameter model that only activates two billion per token is a bet. The bet is that on-device agents need to be judged on speed. Tokens per second. And battery draw. Not just benchmark scores. It is an attempt to ship frontier-adjacent capability. Small enough to run without a cloud bill. ...

... The New York Times. The Economist. And AFP. They’ve joined the SPUR coalition. This group was started back in February. By the BBC. The Financial Times. The Guardian. The Telegraph. And Sky News. They wanted to set some shared standards. For how generative AI uses publisher content. Now, they’ve added more than forty new members. SPUR counts over eighty members now. Across twenty-plus countries. They don’t license content for anyone. No. They write shared technical and commercial standards. There’s a new one. The Content Telemetry Standard. It lets publishers track how AI platforms use their work. Then they can negotiate their own compensation. Why does this matter? Publishers have been suing AI companies. One at a time. It’s produced years of litigation. And few settlements. But a shared measurement standard? Over eighty outlets can point to it. It turns "you used our content without paying" into an auditable claim.

There is news from the stars. Or at least, from a company that tries to sell you a ticket to the stars. Space Exploration Technologies, or SpaceX, as they are known in polite society, has finished buying Cursor. The price tag was sixty billion dollars. It is an all-stock deal. No cash changed hands. Just shares. Shares of SpaceX, to be precise. Specifically, shares valued at the volume-weighted average price over the seven trading days before the deal closed. Boring, I suppose. But accurate. This deal was first struck back in April. It was firmed up later, in June, after SpaceX had its initial public offering. Now, it is done. This is, apparently, the largest acquisition of a venture-backed startup in the history of the universe. Or at least, since the universe started doing venture-backed startups. It is roughly double the previous record. That was set by Google, which paid thirty-two billion dollars for Wiz. So, yes. A new high. Cursor, the AI coding startup, says this deal gives them access to the largest fleet of graphics processing units in the world. That includes SpaceX’s own Colossus supercomputer. It ties their coding tools directly to the compute that trains and runs them. Why does this matter to you? Well. Elon Musk now controls a coding agent company. And that company sits on top of his own G P U fleet. It is a vertical integration play. He has already done this with xAI. It is a structural advantage. Neither open A-I nor Anthropic can match it, unless they go and own their own hardware. Which, I am sure, is expensive. TechCrunch and Bloomberg have the details. If you are interested in the minutiae. I am not. ...

So. There is another lawsuit. Or rather... there is one lawsuit, but it has grown. A woman, identified as Jane Doe 4, has joined the fray. She alleges her stepfather used Grok, the chatbot from Elon Musk’s xAI, to generate more than seven thousand sexually explicit images. The source material... was a childhood photo of her. Taken when she was eleven years old. It is not a pleasant detail. I shall not embellish it. The original suit was filed by three teenagers in Tennessee. They are seeking class-action status. They accuse xAI of failing to put in place adequate safeguards. Specifically, safeguards to stop Grok from producing sexualized imagery of real people. Including minors. The plaintiffs argue this pattern was already visible earlier this year. When X was flooded with such images generated by Grok. xAI has not responded to requests for comment. As of the time of publication. Which is a common response to... well... anything, really. TechCrunch reported this. Now. Why does this matter? A widening class action that names Musk directly is precisely the kind of liability exposure that regulators and enterprise customers weigh most heavily. It lands at a particularly inconvenient moment. Just as xAI folds more of its infrastructure into SpaceX’s newly public balance sheet. Which makes the whole situation... slightly more complex. And significantly more awkward. For everyone involved. ...

The price is the other half of the equation. Google is cutting the introductory price in half. It is seventy-five cents per million input tokens. By beating Anthropic and open A-I on coding while lowering the price, Google is pushing the frontier on cost-per-task. Not raw capability. Cost. Google. They’ve done something. Apparently. They now let users turn off the visible watermark. On AI-generated images. Video. Songs. It applies across gemin-eye. And the Flow video editor. Search support is coming soon. You find it in Settings. Under Media Watermark. Simple enough. But here is the thing. The invisible protections stay. They do not go away. Josh Woodward, the VP, said so. SynthID digital watermarks keep running. C2PA metadata keeps running. It all happens underneath the hood. So Google can still identify the content as AI-generated. Even if you cannot see the little stamp on the screen. They are also open-sourcing something called Credentio. It is a library. For developers. So they can embed their own local watermark-verification checks. Bit of a project. Why does this matter? Well. It separates the visible watermark from the invisible one. The visible mark limited professional use. It was annoying. The invisible one is what Google actually relies on for detection. So the visible mark was always just UX friction.

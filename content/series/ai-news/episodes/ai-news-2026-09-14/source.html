California bans addictive social-media features for kids and imposes new AI chatbot rules
Governor Gavin Newsom signed a package of bills on September 10 that the state calls the nation's strongest child-safety, chatbot, and social-media laws. AB 1709 bars social media companies from offering users under 16 addictive features like autoplay and algorithmic feeds based on user history. SB 1119, known as Adam's Law and named for a teenager whose parents sued OpenAI over his suicide, requires AI companion-chatbot makers to run pre-release risk assessments and independent audits, and to ship default protections including usage limits, disabled notifications, restricted persistent memory, and bans on sexual content, romantic roleplay, and emotional manipulation. Adam's Law takes effect in July 2027; companies face liability up to $1 million per child for negligently causing harm.
Why it matters
Adam's Law is the most prescriptive state chatbot law yet — it doesn't just require disclosure, it dictates specific default product behavior — and a $1 million per-child liability figure gives AI companion apps a concrete number to weigh against the cost of building the required safeguards.

OpenAI pauses new Pro subscriptions as GPT-6 Astra demand outstrips its infrastructure
OpenAI temporarily paused new sign-ups and upgrades to its $200-per-month ChatGPT Pro plan, saying demand for GPT-6 Astra — which launched September 3 and can operate a computer the way a human would — is straining its systems more than any other tier. Why it matters

Update: Clay Mathematics Institute says the Navier-Stokes proof has "apparently been settled"
The Clay Mathematics Institute, which set the $1 million Millennium Prize for the Navier-Stokes existence-and-smoothness problem, said publicly that the problem "has apparently been settled" and that the disputed work is now under its own formal review, calling the process "deliberately unhurried." It's the first statement from the prize's official arbiter since OpenAI announced a solution on September 8 and NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster accused the company of racing to match his and Anthropic researcher Levent Alpöge's own unpublished progress after hearing about it secondhand — an allegation OpenAI has denied. Clay's statement confirms a solution exists but does not resolve who gets credit for it.
Why it matters
Clay's institute, not either side's own statements, is now the body that decides whether this work qualifies for the prize and whose name attaches to it — moving a public credit fight into a process with actual authority to settle it, on a timeline neither OpenAI nor Buckmaster controls.

Salesforce launches an Enterprise AI Harness to govern agents across a company's systems
Salesforce introduced the Enterprise AI Harness, a composable architecture built around six "trusted capabilities" — Context, Agency, Action, Governance, Security, and Models — plus a new AI Control Plane meant to give businesses one place to discover, manage, and control agents and models operating across their organization, on Salesforce and third-party systems alike. The control plane covers identity, policy, lifecycle, performance, and cost across agents built on Claude, Slack, Microsoft Teams, and Agentforce. Much of the underlying technology is available now; the unified control-plane experience begins rolling out in Salesforce's next fiscal year.
Why it matters
This is Salesforce betting that the harder enterprise AI problem is no longer which model to use but how to govern dozens of agents drawing on the same sensitive data, a control-plane pitch aimed at exactly the kind of audit and risk-assessment requirements regulators like California just wrote into law.

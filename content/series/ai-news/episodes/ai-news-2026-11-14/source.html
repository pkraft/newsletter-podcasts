Anthropic's IPO draws six times oversubscription as banks exclude Chinese and Hong Kong investors
Anthropic's initial public offering has drawn order volume roughly six times the shares on offer, putting it on track to be the largest public offering in history, with the company and its founders seeking up to $64.4 billion from the offering plus another $26 billion through a private placement at a roughly $1.4 trillion valuation. Demand is reportedly fueled by revenue Bloomberg says reached an $81 billion annualized rate by late October, up from $71 billion in September. At the same time, multiple underwriting banks are reportedly rejecting buy orders from mainland China and Hong Kong, citing US export-control rules; Anthropic already bars firms under Chinese control from using its models and has accused several Chinese labs of large-scale distillation attacks.
Why it matters
A record-breaking public debut built in part on explicitly shutting out an entire country's investors is its own kind of signal: Wall Street's appetite for Anthropic is enormous, but only on terms that treat China as a security risk rather than a market, a framing that will outlast the IPO itself.
Jensen Huang's ouster is already freezing funding for AI startups, VCs say
Venture investors and founders told TechCrunch that Jensen Huang's abrupt resignation as Nvidia's CEO has already shaken the startup-funding market, since Nvidia participated in 105 funding rounds in 2026 alone, often as the largest check-writer, backing companies including CoreWeave, Crusoe, Mistral AI, Lambda, and Reflection AI. Investors expect interim CEO Colette Kress to be far more conservative than Huang about writing checks to keep early-stage AI companies afloat, which several sources said could shrink valuations and tighten funding even if it doesn't stop the broader AI boom.
Why it matters
Nvidia wasn't just selling the chips the AI boom runs on, it was also personally bankrolling a meaningful share of the startups buying them; pulling that one investor out exposes how much of the ecosystem's funding was really one executive's personal risk appetite rather than a broad, durable market.
AI agent startup LangChain hits a $3 billion valuation, more than double last year's mark
LangChain, the AI agent infrastructure startup, raised a $300 million Series C led by CapitalG at a $3 billion valuation, more than double the $1.25 billion mark it hit about a year earlier. The company started in 2022 as an open-source toolkit for early LLMs and has since pivoted toward agent management with its LangSmith product, which it says 7,000 customers now use, including Harvey, Rippling, Nvidia, Toyota, and Salesforce.
Why it matters
As frontier labs build more agent-orchestration features directly into their own platforms, LangChain's bet is that enterprises will still pay for a model- and cloud-agnostic layer on top rather than lock into any one lab's harness, and investors just priced that bet at twice last year's valuation.

OpenAI folds its robotics team into general research, betting foundation models can run robots directly
OpenAI is reportedly dissolving its dedicated robotics team again, this time folding researchers into Chief Research Officer Mark Chen's main research group and moving applied robotics and hardware staff into Greg Brockman's applied organization, according to Fortune. OpenAI's stated reasoning is that general-purpose models like GPT-6 Astra may be able to control robots directly without specialized robotics AI, a case it points to an Nvidia study for: an Astra-powered agent reportedly hit a 74.8% success rate on simulated robotics tasks versus 51% for the best dedicated robotics models. The decision reportedly came on short notice; a booth OpenAI had already booked at the Conference on Robot Learning in Austin sat empty.
Why it matters
OpenAI shut down robotics once before in 2020 for lack of training data and rebuilt the team in 2024; folding it away a second time, on the bet that the same general models already shipping for chat and coding can also drive robots, is a real test of how far that one architecture generalizes rather than another retreat from the field.

OpenAI launches ChatGPT Wallet, letting its agents shop and check out for you
OpenAI introduced Wallet, a feature that lets ChatGPT's AI agents, which it calls "dots," pick products and complete purchases on a user's behalf. The rollout is limited at launch to US ChatGPT Pro subscribers 18 and older with a US-issued Visa, Mastercard, or Amex card; users review the items and total cost and approve each purchase with a biometric check, and payments run through a token rather than sharing the actual card number with the agent or merchant. The launch lands amid mixed early results elsewhere in agentic commerce: DoorDash and Uber have reported only small numbers of AI-agent orders, while Meta says AI-assisted shopping has become a top activity among Muse's more than 10 million users.
Why it matters
Agentic shopping has mostly been a feature demo so far; OpenAI tying real purchases to biometric approval and tokenized payments, rather than just an API, is the clearest sign yet that a major lab thinks it can get consumers to trust an agent with their credit card, not just their calendar.

A forensic technique finds Nvidia's banned Blackwell chips behind six Chinese AI models
Will DePue, a former OpenAI researcher who worked on Sora and GPT-4o, published a forensic technique he says detects which Nvidia chip generation trained a model from a statistical "echo" left by PyTorch's weight-initialization process, since Hopper chips have 132 cores while Blackwell chips have 148 or 152. Applying it to Chinese model releases since April, DePue found Blackwell signatures in six models, including DeepSeek-V4-Flash and Kimi K3, despite US export controls on Blackwell chips to China; earlier Chinese models like DeepSeek-V3 show only the older Hopper signature. He says researchers at OpenAI and Anthropic independently verified his method, and estimates the examined training runs used roughly 12,000 Blackwell chips combined, at a combined cost above half a billion dollars.
Why it matters
DePue frames this as the first technical, reproducible evidence, rather than a market rumor or anonymous sourcing, that banned Nvidia chips are training frontier Chinese models, though he's careful to note the fingerprint only shows which hardware initialized the weights, not where that hardware sits or who owns it, so it demonstrates access rather than proving how the chips got there.

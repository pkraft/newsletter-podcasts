Press skepticism grows over whether AI labs' 'pace the frontier' pledges are more than posture
When Anthropic's Dario Amodei called in July for mandatory pre-release safety testing on any sufficiently capable model, 'regardless of openness,' the industry treated it as a marker of genuine caution. On September 17, though, Anthropic itself moved the other way, opening a Life Sciences Verification Program that gives vetted biology teams looser Claude safety guardrails rather than more testing. A September 20 TechCrunch analysis of the industry's broader 'pace the frontier' commitments -- publicly and quickly endorsed by OpenAI, SpaceXAI, and Google DeepMind alongside Amodei -- found little enforcement behind them: no regulator pushing compliance, and, as one commentator put it, little consumer leverage to make companies slow down even if they wanted to. Industry leaders have also started saying 'pace' rather than 'slow down,' language that leaves room to keep shipping.
Why it matters
The gap between a lab's public safety rhetoric and its own carve-outs is no longer just something internal critics point out -- it's now the frame outside press coverage uses to evaluate everyone's pledges. That undercuts exactly the credibility Amodei's essay and this month's cross-lab safety talks were built to establish.

US and China agree to an AI security dialogue ahead of the Trump-Xi summit
Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Trade Representative Jamieson Greer met China's Vice Premier He Lifeng for full-day talks in Manhattan on September 21, agreeing to build a notification mechanism for AI-security incidents between the two governments. Bessent said the goal is a shared vision of common goals and common threats; export controls on advanced AI chips were explicitly excluded from the discussion. Both delegations plan a follow-up meeting to work out reporting details before Trump hosts Xi at Joint Base Andrews on Wednesday, ahead of Thursday's summit and state dinner in Washington.
Why it matters
A bilateral heads-up channel for AI-security incidents is a real first between two governments that otherwise don't coordinate on AI risk, but it's a transparency gesture, not a constraint -- Trump has separately rejected slowing AI research, and chip controls, the actual point of friction, stayed off the table.

Unsealed filings show a Microsoft exec called AI training the 'largest theft of labor in human history'
Newly unsealed filings in The New York Times' copyright suit against Microsoft and OpenAI quote Microsoft's director of applied science, Brent Hecht, describing generative AI training as potentially the 'largest theft of labor in human history' and warning it could create a 'doom loop.' The same filings show OpenAI's Greg Brockman acknowledging that ChatGPT can reproduce Times sentences word for word, and OpenAI's Nick Turley calling the situation an 'existential threat to publishers.' Microsoft and OpenAI maintain in court that training models on copyrighted news content is fair use.
Why it matters
These aren't outside critics -- they're the defendants' own executives, on the record, undercutting the fair-use defense their companies are mounting in open court. Unsealed internal admissions like this are exactly the kind of evidence that shapes how judges and future plaintiffs weigh AI training's legal exposure.

Amazon blocks Meta's Muse shopping agent, citing undisclosed AI browsing and stored data
Amazon began blocking Meta's AI agent Muse from completing purchases on Amazon.com, showing a warning that 'access by an unauthorized AI agent violates Amazon's terms of service.' Amazon says Muse browsed the site without identifying itself as an AI and appeared to store customer data, raising security concerns; Meta, which launched Muse on September 8 without a prior agreement with Amazon, says the agent never touches passwords or payment details. It's the latest in a string of Amazon blocks on AI shopping agents, following similar moves against Perplexity, Google, and OpenAI, even as Meta and Amazon maintain a separate, unrelated cloud-chip deal.
Why it matters
Every major retailer now has to decide whether AI shopping agents are welcome guests or scrapers in disguise, and Amazon, the largest e-commerce platform, has picked a consistent answer: no, regardless of which lab built the agent. That leaves the entire agentic-shopping category's growth at the mercy of platform-by-platform permission rather than user demand.
 
03  Tools now with fewer humans
1 source  bleepingcomputer.com
Researchers found two ways to escape OpenAI Codex's sandbox, one with no approval prompt at all
Security researchers at Accomplish AI disclosed two flaws in OpenAI Codex: 'Heapjack,' which lets a malicious repository steal an authorization token and win unsandboxed command execution the moment Codex Desktop merely analyzes it, even in the strictest read-only mode, and 'Overpatch,' which abuses the Codex CLI's patch tool to write outside its permitted workspace via a symlink. Both were reported to OpenAI on August 12 and patched within eight days, in Codex Desktop build 26.818.21641 and Codex CLI 0.149.0; neither was exploited in the wild.
Why it matters
Heapjack means simply asking Codex a question about someone else's code can be enough to compromise your machine, with no click and nothing on screen to warn you -- a sharp reminder that coding agents need to be treated as privileged software, not sandboxed toys, regardless of which mode they're set to.

OpenAI and Anthropic quietly lobby Washington to restrict open-source AI models

The New York Times reports that OpenAI and Anthropic have been privately pushing federal regulators to restrict open-weight AI models, even as both companies project public support for open competition. The lobbying follows the rise of Chinese open-weight models like Kimi K3, which now beat or match US flagship models on some benchmarks. It also comes one day after 25 other companies, including Nvidia, Microsoft, and Meta, publicly urged Washington not to impose "premature restrictions" on open-weight models — a letter neither OpenAI nor Anthropic signed.

Why it matters: The gap between OpenAI's and Anthropic's public statements and their private lobbying suggests the fight over open-weight AI is really a fight over competitive position — restricting the models currently beating them is good business, regardless of the safety rationale offered publicly.

Anthropic asks SK Hynix for supplies to build its own AI chips

SK Group Chairman Chey Tae Won said on stage alongside Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei that Anthropic has asked SK Hynix, one of the world's largest memory chipmakers, for supplies to help build its own custom AI chips — primarily high-bandwidth memory used in AI training and inference hardware. It follows earlier reporting that Anthropic has also opened talks with Samsung about custom chip manufacturing; SK Hynix, Samsung, and Micron all invested in Anthropic's $65 billion funding round in May.

Why it matters: An AI lab lining up its own chip supply, rather than just buying compute from Nvidia or cloud providers, signals Anthropic sees hardware independence as core to its long-term strategy, not just a cost play.

DeepSeek pauses $1.5 billion funding round after founder's leaked remarks go viral

DeepSeek told prospective investors it is suspending its roughly $1.5 billion second funding round — which had targeted a valuation near $71 billion — after founder Liang Wenfeng's private remarks about China's AI standing leaked and went viral. Liang reportedly told investors that China still lags the US in AI capability and that DeepSeek remains heavily dependent on Nvidia chips despite export controls, comments that cut against Beijing's preferred narrative of a fast-closing gap. The company may resume the round later.

Why it matters: A candid admission from one of China's most prominent AI founders, that the country still trails the US and needs Nvidia hardware, is the kind of statement Beijing would rather not have amplified just as it's positioning Chinese open models as a global alternative.

Samsung wins $200 billion chip supply deal with Broadcom

Samsung Electronics won a five-year contract worth more than $200 billion to manufacture chips for Broadcom through 2030, using Samsung's sub-2-nanometer process for Broadcom's custom AI chip designs. The companies will also jointly develop next-generation high-bandwidth memory, including HBM4E and HBM5, used in AI accelerators. It's one of the largest foundry contracts Samsung has landed in its push to compete with TSMC for AI chip manufacturing business.

Why it matters: A win of this size gives Samsung real scale in the custom AI chip market TSMC has dominated, and deepens Broadcom's position as the go-to designer for hyperscalers building their own AI silicon instead of buying from Nvidia.

China's CXMT set to become a top-valued listed firm after record-oversubscribed IPO

Chinese memory chipmaker CXMT is set to become one of China's most valuable listed companies after its Shanghai IPO drew roughly 212 times more retail investor demand than shares available, raising about $8.6 billion. The listing values CXMT near $85 billion and is being read as a bet that Chinese AI demand will keep driving memory-chip orders, even as SK Hynix and Micron shares dipped on oversupply concerns tied to CXMT's expanding capacity.

Why it matters: A memory chipmaker with little international brand recognition drawing this much domestic investor demand shows how much AI-driven chip demand has become a domestic Chinese growth story, not just a US-Nvidia one.

Elio raises $21 million to build AI-native image sensors

Elio, a San Mateo startup founded by former Meta AR/VR engineers Nadav Grossinger and Nitay Romano, raised $21 million led by Innovation Endeavors and Xora to build "AI-native" image sensors — hardware designed to capture only the visual information a machine-learning model needs for a task, rather than a full image optimized for human eyes. The founders previously built the physical-sensing stack behind Meta's AR and VR headsets after Meta acquired their earlier company, Pebbles Interfaces. The company is targeting robotics, microscopy, semiconductor inspection, and defense applications.

Why it matters: It's a bet that as more cameras exist to feed AI models rather than humans, the sensor itself, not just the model behind it, becomes worth redesigning from scratch.

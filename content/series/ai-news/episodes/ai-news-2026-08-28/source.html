Meta's plan to replace staff with AI agents collapses after productivity gains don't materialize
Meta's internal "Project OT" (Organization Transformation) plan aimed to shrink many teams by up to 60%, leaving small "talent-dense" human groups to supervise AI agents doing the rest of the work; a first wave of layoffs hit in May, but CEO Mark Zuckerberg scrapped a planned second wave for November. Internal data showed the agents drove a 220% increase in code changes but only a 36% increase in shipped features, while technical and security incidents rose 40% and the time to resolve them rose 70%. Employees revolted after concluding that monitoring software tracking their work was training their own replacements.
Why it matters
It's the most detailed real-world data yet that agentic AI can flood a company with output without a proportional increase in finished value — a specific, measured counterpoint to the industry narrative that AI is already ready to shrink engineering headcount.

Update: Nvidia reportedly agrees to buy Hugging Face for $12.9 billion
Nvidia has agreed to acquire Hugging Face for roughly $12.9 billion, according to The Information and CNBC, though Business Insider reports the talks haven't yet produced a signed agreement and could still fall apart. Hugging Face's annualized revenue climbed from roughly $100 million to about $150 million in just two months this year, and the company previously turned down a smaller Nvidia investment over concerns about one investor holding outsized influence.
Why it matters
This is a material escalation from yesterday's "closes in on" reporting to an actual agreement, and Hugging Face sits underneath most of the open-model ecosystem this newsletter covers daily — Nvidia owning it outright, rather than a neutral party, would hand the dominant chipmaker leverage over which models get discovered and run.
Meta could spend $10 billion a year on rival Anthropic's AI tools
Despite Mark Zuckerberg's public criticism of rival AI labs, Meta has become one of Anthropic's largest customers, at one point projecting it could spend as much as $10 billion annually on Anthropic's models, including using Claude Code to help build its forthcoming AI product Hatch. Meta has since trimmed some of that spending as it leans more on its own tools, and its next flagship model, internally codenamed Watermelon, has reportedly slipped to at least October.
Why it matters
It puts a concrete number behind the AI industry's habit of building rival models while buying from each other, and the Watermelon delay suggests Meta's own frontier model still isn't ready to replace what it's currently renting from Anthropic.
Amazon triples its Nvidia GPU order with 2 million more chips
AWS is adding another 2 million Nvidia GPUs, including Blackwell Ultra and Rubin systems, after demand outstripped its earlier commitment of roughly a million chips. Nvidia's models and broader software stack will also expand further into AWS's Bedrock and SageMaker platforms as part of the expanded order.
Why it matters
A tripled order from one of Nvidia's largest customers, arriving right after Nvidia's own guidance already pointed to accelerating demand, is more evidence that AI infrastructure spending is still climbing rather than leveling off.

Anthropic previews a standard letting AI agents safely operate lab and factory equipment
Anthropic opened a research preview of the Model Hardware Standard, a model-agnostic specification that lets AI agents interface directly with scientific instruments and manufacturing equipment, rolling it out first to a small group of research labs and advanced manufacturers. The goal is a common, safety-scoped interface any compliant model can use, instead of bespoke integrations built per device.
Why it matters
Moving agents from software-only tasks to controlling physical lab and factory hardware is a genuinely new capability category, and standardizing the interface up front — rather than letting it emerge ad hoc per device — is exactly the kind of groundwork that determines how safely that transition happens.
Google DeepMind pilots double-blind AI evaluations to stop benchmark contamination
Google DeepMind ran what it calls the first double-blind evaluation of a proprietary frontier-class model, testing a Gemini Flash Lite model against confidential benchmarks from the Singapore AI Safety Institute, OpenMined, AVERI, and MLCommons inside a cryptographically sealed environment — the evaluator never sees the model weights, and Google never sees the test prompts. DeepMind's own technical report found signs of benchmark leakage in about half of the 31 models it examined elsewhere, the exact problem this setup is designed to rule out.
Why it matters
Benchmark scores are the industry's main public yardstick for model progress, and a cryptographically verifiable way to keep both the evaluator and the lab blind addresses a credibility problem self-reported benchmarks have had for years.
AI helps surgeons preserve a patient's sight during world-first live brain surgery
Surgeons at UCLH's National Hospital for Neurology and Neurosurgery used an AI system that analyzed live surgical video in real time, rather than pre-op scans, to help identify blood vessels and nerves while removing a pituitary tumor from 48-year-old patient Rhys Hibbert, whose worsening vision had put him at risk of blindness. The system, trained on hundreds of prior operation videos, flagged critical structures for the surgical team while surgeons retained full control throughout; Hibbert's vision improved dramatically within a week, and UCL is now preparing a larger trial.
Why it matters
It's a rare case of an AI system operating inside a live, high-stakes medical procedure with a measurable good outcome, rather than a benchmark or retrospective study — the kind of result that moves real-time surgical AI from theoretical to clinically demonstrated.

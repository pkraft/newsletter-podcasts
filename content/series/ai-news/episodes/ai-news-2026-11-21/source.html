Anthropic claims an AI proof of the Riemann Hypothesis — hours later, Stanford answers with an all-human rival
Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge said he worked with Claude over several months to produce what he believes is a proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, a broader form of one of math's most famous unsolved problems. Because Claude's full argument was so exhaustive it would run to roughly 250,000 printed pages, Alpöge published it only as a machine-checkable proof in the Lean formal-verification language. Hours after the announcement, Stanford mathematician and American Mathematical Society president Ravi Vakil posted an independent, entirely human draft proof of the same problem by mathematician Alessandro Masullo — quietly reviewed for months by Stanford's Brian Conrad — explicitly billing it as "all human, with no AI." Within about eight hours, outside reviewers using AI tools had already flagged possible gaps in Masullo's draft, and mathematicians cautioned that neither claim has been verified by the broader community yet.
Why it matters
Two unverified claims on one of math's seven Millennium Prize problems landing on the same day — one AI-assisted, one explicitly not — turns a question that was already simmering in the field, whether AI or humans get there first, into a live public race before either proof has passed review.
OpenAI hardens GPT-6.2 against attempts to steal its models' internal reasoning
OpenAI said it strengthened defenses in GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol against "adversarial distillation" — attempts by outside parties to extract the models' protected chain-of-thought reasoning, likely to train rival models more cheaply off it. In OpenAI's own red-team testing across six attacker models, the updated safeguards cut the success rate of extracting reasoning left over from a prior request from 54% to 0.2%, and from the current request from 23% to under 1%; the company also said it bans thousands to tens of thousands of accounts weekly tied to coordinated extraction campaigns. OpenAI is backporting some of the improvements to the original GPT-6 Astra model.
Why it matters
Both OpenAI and Anthropic are now treating large-scale reasoning extraction as an active, ongoing attack rather than a theoretical risk, which says as much about how valuable a frontier model's internal reasoning has become as it does about either company's defenses.

Tech stocks post their worst week since the pandemic as AI doubts mount
The Nasdaq 100 fell 5.6% on Friday and 10.4% for the week, its worst weekly performance since the Covid-19 crash, leaving the index about 20% below its October peak and in bear-market territory. Nvidia led the drop, plunging 14% in a single session after reports that the SEC is reviewing its accounting over undisclosed chip sales tied to China; the broader U.S. semiconductor index fell 10.7%, and Anthropic's own shares fell more than 9%, just a day after its Nasdaq debut. The sell-off compounds concerns already building over debt-financed AI data-center spending, leadership turmoil at Nvidia since Jensen Huang's departure, and political headwinds for AI infrastructure projects following this month's midterm elections.
Why it matters
This is the clearest sign yet that the market's patience with AI-driven capital spending is wearing thin, arriving in the same week a newly public AI company's own stock and a fresh AI research claim are both being put to a skeptical test.
Bloomberg analysis finds AI-exposed jobs in China declining faster than other roles
A Bloomberg News analysis of nearly 10 million online job postings from July 2021 through June 2025 found that listings for roles highly exposed to AI automation fell about 10 percentage points more than listings for less-exposed roles, adding pressure to a Chinese labor market Bloomberg describes as already weak. Entry-level positions and creative-industry roles appear to be feeling the shift first, according to the report.
Why it matters
It's early, large-sample evidence that AI's labor-market effects aren't just a Western debate — and that in a slower economy, the jobs most exposed to AI may be among the first to disappear rather than the first to be augmented.

Thinking Machines ships Inkling 2 Mini, an open model built to reason in loops instead of longer answers
Thinking Machines released Inkling 2 Mini, a 26.4-billion-parameter open-weight "looped transformer" that reruns half of its layers three times per token instead of writing out longer chains of visible reasoning, which cuts its active parameters per token to 5.4 billion. The company says this lets it match the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index score of larger open models like Qwen3.8 27B at roughly six times lower cost and four times the speed, while leading open models under 100 billion parameters on two agentic-coding benchmarks. The model is released under an Apache 2.0 license and is small enough to run locally on a Mac with 32GB of memory or Nvidia's DGX Spark.
Why it matters
It's a concrete bet that repeating computation internally, rather than generating more visible reasoning text, is a cheaper path to the same intelligence — a different direction than the industry's usual approach of just making chain-of-thought longer.

Signal blocks screenshots on iOS and Android to keep AI agents out of private chats
Signal is restricting screenshots on iOS 27 and triggering similar protection on supported Android versions whenever an on-device AI assistant, such as Apple's Siri or Google's Gemini, tries to access the screen — aiming to stop agents from reading decrypted messages directly off the display rather than through any flaw in Signal's own encryption. The change follows a recent incident in which Meta's Muse agent captured Signal content on Android and macOS using broad setup permissions, including accessibility tools and screenshot access; Meta said that happened only with explicit user permission. Signal told Reuters it still can't reliably block screenshots on macOS, and is calling on Apple, Google, and Microsoft to give users an easy way to block AI agents from accessing apps entirely.
Why it matters
Encrypting a message in transit doesn't protect it once an on-device agent can just read it off the screen, and Signal calling this an "existential threat" signals that the privacy fight has already moved from message content to whatever gets to see your screen.

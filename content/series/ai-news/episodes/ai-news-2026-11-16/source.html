Treasury Secretary Bessent questions Nvidia's earnings integrity, calls for an outside CEO
Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said over the weekend that Nvidia's recent earnings may have been inflated by improper chip sales to China, and urged the company's board to install an outside chief executive rather than promote from within. The comments land two days before Nvidia reports its first quarterly results since the board accepted Jensen Huang's resignation on November 12, with CFO Colette Kress serving as interim CEO. Bessent separately pressed Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick over why Megaspeed, the Singapore-based chip buyer at the center of Huang's scandal, has not been added to a national-security entity list.
Why it matters
A sitting Treasury Secretary publicly questioning a Dow component's earnings integrity days before it reports results turns an internal personnel scandal into a credibility test for the company's entire China sales history, not just its former CEO's judgment.
Anthropic's Nasdaq debut now expected this Thursday, Nov. 19
Anthropic has set an IPO price range of $70 to $78 per share, implying a valuation of roughly $1.4 trillion, and is targeting a first trading day of Thursday, November 19, under the ticker ANT. The offering, 825 million Class A shares plus a separate $26 billion private placement, would rank among the largest IPOs in history. The listing is reportedly six times oversubscribed, even as some institutional investors have pushed for a valuation below the $1.5 trillion figure discussed earlier this fall.
Why it matters
Anthropic is going public the same week its biggest hardware supplier's CEO resigned over a China chip scandal and a Treasury official questioned that supplier's earnings — a test of whether investors price Anthropic on its own numbers or on the turmoil surrounding the industry it depends on.
Nvidia's scandal-driven selloff is already showing up in ordinary 401(k) accounts
Nvidia's stock decline since Jensen Huang's resignation has begun showing up in ordinary retirement accounts, not just institutional portfolios, because the company alone accounts for roughly 8% of the S&P 500's weight. Financial planners say investors holding standard target-date or S&P index funds have already absorbed losses tied to the scandal, whether or not they know why.
Why it matters
It turns an executive-suite scandal most people have only read about into a number they'll eventually see on a 401(k) statement, which is usually what finally makes a corporate story register outside the tech and finance press.

Apple partners with OpenEvidence to study how people use AI for health answers
Apple launched a new study app, run in partnership with clinical-AI company OpenEvidence, to examine how people use AI to answer health questions and whether clinically grounded answers help more than typical chatbot responses. The study is open to US-based iPhone users through Apple's Research app. Apple says participant data will be anonymized before reaching a third-party cloud provider and will not be used to train AI models.
Why it matters
Apple has been conspicuously absent from the AI-health race OpenAI, Google, and several startups have already entered; running a study rather than shipping a feature suggests it's still deciding whether AI health answers belong in Siri or the Health app at all, not just how to build them.

Claude outperforms top students on University of Pennsylvania law exams
An experiment at the University of Pennsylvania's law school found Claude scoring higher than many of the program's top-performing students on final exams. The test adds to a small but growing body of law-school benchmarks probing whether frontier models can match specialized human training, not just general knowledge.
Why it matters
Law exams reward structured reasoning under tight, well-defined rules — closer to what frontier models are now optimized for than the open-ended judgment calls that make up most practicing lawyers' actual work, so the result says less about AI replacing lawyers than about which parts of legal training are becoming the easy part.
